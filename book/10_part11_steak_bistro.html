Nine cuts, three methods, and one rule that matters more than all of them: thickness decides the method, not the name on the label.

Thickness decides the method

A steak under 1½ inches thick should be cooked hot and fast: there is not enough mass for the surface and centre to fall out of step. A steak over 1½ inches should be reverse seared, because by the time a fierce fire has browned the outside it has cooked an inch of grey band underneath it. That's the whole decision. The name on the package is irrelevant to it.

Salting windows

There are only two acceptable times to salt a steak, and the gap between them is the problem.

  • Immediately before cooking — under three minutes. The salt has not yet drawn moisture to the surface, so the crust forms normally.
  • At least 45 minutes before, ideally 24 hours — the drawn-out moisture has dissolved the salt and been reabsorbed, carrying seasoning inward, and the surface has dried again. This is better, and for anything over 1½ inches it is the only right answer.

Between those windows — say, twenty minutes ahead — the surface is wet with brine and will steam rather than sear. Use 1 teaspoon of kosher salt per pound and leave the steak uncovered on a rack in the fridge.

Probe through the side, horizontally into the centre, not down from the top — a vertical probe passes through hot outer layers and reads high. On a porterhouse, probe the filet side, which cooks faster and decides the steak.

Resting

Half the cooking time, loosely tented, never wrapped tight. A steak cut immediately loses juice to the board; one rested too long under foil steams its own crust soft. For a 1½-inch steak, eight minutes. For a côte de boeuf, fifteen.

Hot and Fast
Method · Any steak under 1½ inches · 10 min
  • Steaks, 1–1½ inches thick
  • Coarse salt and coarse black pepper
  • 1 tbsp neutral oil or beef tallow
  • Butter, thyme, garlic, to finish
  1. Salt in one of the two windows above. Bring fully to room temperature — 45 minutes out of the fridge.
  2. Build a two-zone fire, the hot side as fierce as a full chimney of lump allows.
  3. Oil the steak, not the grate. Pat the surface dry first; a wet steak will not brown.
  4. Sear 2 to 3 minutes per side, turning once. If flare-ups come, move to the cool zone until they die and return — never spray water.
  5. Stand the steak on its fat edge with tongs for 30 seconds to render it.
  6. Pull at 128°F. Rest 8 minutes with butter, thyme and a crushed garlic clove on top.
  7. Slice across the grain, or serve whole for a thick single-portion cut.
The Reverse Sear
Method · Any steak over 1½ inches · 1 hr

Low heat first, fire last. It gives a far wider band of evenly pink meat and a better crust, because the surface is bone dry by the time it meets the coals.

  • Steaks or roasts, 1½–3 inches thick
  • Coarse salt, applied 24 hours ahead
  • Oil or tallow for the sear
  1. Dry-brine uncovered in the fridge overnight.
  2. Deflector in, kettle at 225°F, steak on the indirect side with a probe in the centre.
  3. Bring it up to 15°F below your target — for medium rare, 115°F. This takes 35 to 75 minutes depending on thickness.
  4. Rest for 10 minutes while you pull the deflector and build a fierce bed of coals. This rest is not optional: it lets the surface dry, which is most of why the crust is better.
  5. Sear 60 to 90 seconds per side over maximum heat. You are browning, not cooking.
  6. Pull at 128–130°F. Rest only 5 to 8 minutes — it has already rested once.
Filet Mignon with Périgueux
Main · Serves 4 · 20 min

The honest case for filet: it is the tenderest cut and the least flavourful, which is exactly why the classical repertoire built its best sauces around it. Cooked without one, it is a waste of money. Cooked with Périgueux or béarnaise, it is the most elegant thing on a grill.

  • 4 filet mignon steaks, 6–8 oz each, 1½–2 inches thick
  • 4 rashers streaky bacon or thin pancetta, for barding (optional but recommended)
  • Coarse salt and coarse black pepper
  • 2 tbsp tallow or oil · 3 tbsp butter · thyme
  • Sauce Périgueux or béarnaise (Chapters 51 and 52)
  1. Dry-brine 12 to 24 hours. Filet has almost no fat, so the seasoning has to come from inside.
  2. Bard it: wrap a rasher of bacon around the circumference and tie with twine. This protects the sides, adds the fat the cut lacks, and keeps the round shape. Remove the twine before serving; the bacon can stay or go.
  3. Two-zone fire, fierce hot side. Sear all round — 2 minutes per face, turning through four quarter-turns, then briefly on each flat end.
  4. Move to indirect and bring to 125–128°F, 5 to 8 minutes. Filet is the one cut worth taking to rare rather than medium rare.
  5. Rest 8 minutes with butter and thyme.
  6. Spoon the sauce onto warm plates first and set the steak on top — never over it.
To the table · plateThe one properly plated steak in this book. Sauce pooled on a warm plate, the filet stood upright on it so the height reads, a few grains of flaky salt on the meat and nothing on the sauce. Everything else — potatoes, greens — comes in separate dishes so the plate stays spare.
Picanha on the Rotisserie
Main · Serves 6–8 · 1 hr · Rotisserie

The Brazilian churrasco cut: top sirloin cap with the fat left on, cut into thick strips, folded into C-shapes on a skewer so the fat cap is always on the outside and bastes everything below it.

  • 1 whole picanha, 2½–3 lb, fat cap intact and untrimmed
  • Coarse rock salt — a generous handful, nothing else
  1. Do not trim the fat cap. Score it in a diamond pattern, cutting into the fat but not the meat.
  2. Cut the picanha with the grain into strips about 1½ inches wide — this is the one time you cut with the grain, because you will slice across it later.
  3. Bend each strip into a C with the fat on the outer curve and thread onto the spit or a flat skewer, packed tight against each other.
  4. Coat the fat heavily with coarse rock salt, pressing it in. Knock the excess off before it goes on.
  5. Rotisserie at 350°F for 45 to 60 minutes to 130°F in the centre of the thickest piece.
  6. The churrasco method: shave the browned outer layer off with a long knife as it cooks, serve it immediately, and return the rest to the fire — like the al pastor trompo, in beef.
  7. Or take the whole thing off, rest 10 minutes, and slice each C across the grain so every slice has a strip of fat along its edge.
To the table · boardShaved straight onto a board or onto plates held out by guests, in rounds, with nothing but coarse salt and farofa if you have it. If you are slicing rather than shaving, fan the slices fat-edge-up so the golden cap is visible along the whole row.
Hanger and Bavette
Main · Serves 6 · 15 min

The two best-value steaks on the animal. Both have an aggressively open grain, which means they drink marinade and must be sliced across the grain — get that wrong and they are inedible, get it right and they beat a sirloin.

  • 2 lb hanger steak, central sinew removed, or 2 lb bavette
  • ⅓ cup olive oil · 4 cloves garlic, crushed
  • 2 tbsp red wine vinegar · 1 tbsp Dijon
  • 2 sprigs thyme · coarse salt and pepper
  • Chimichurri (Chapter 22) or marchand de vin butter (Chapter 52)
  1. Hanger has a tough central sinew running its length — have the butcher remove it, or cut the steak into two lobes either side of it yourself.
  2. Marinate 2 to 6 hours. Both cuts take marinade unusually well because of the open grain.
  3. Pat dry, salt, and sear over a fierce fire 3 to 4 minutes per side. Both cuts are irregular in thickness — cook to the thick end and accept that the thin end is more done.
  4. Pull at 128°F. Rest 8 minutes.
  5. Find the grain before you cut. It runs lengthwise and is plainly visible. Slice thinly at 45 degrees across it.
Porterhouse for Two
Main · Serves 2–3 · 45 min · Reverse sear

Two steaks sharing a bone, and they cook at different rates — the filet side is smaller, leaner and finishes several minutes earlier. Positioning solves it.

  • 1 porterhouse, 2–2½ inches thick, 2½–3 lb
  • Coarse salt, 24 hours ahead · coarse black pepper
  • Oil, butter, rosemary · lemon
  1. Dry-brine overnight, uncovered.
  2. Indirect at 225°F with the filet side angled away from the coals and the strip side nearer. This single positioning choice is the whole technique.
  3. Bring up to 110°F at the filet, about 35 minutes. Rest 10.
  4. Sear over a fierce bed, 90 seconds per side, holding it upright on the bone edge for a final 30 seconds.
  5. Pull at 125°F at the filet. Rest 10 minutes.
  6. Carve both muscles off the bone in one piece each, slice across the grain, and lay them back either side of the bone to serve.

Bistro Classique

Winter · Dinner party · Two kettles · Deflector + pizza stone

Everything a Paris bistro does well, moved outdoors: gougères off the stone, onion soup gratinéed under coals, two steaks with two sauces, frites double-fried in a Dutch oven, and a tarte Tatin caramelised in a skillet. It is the most classically technical menu in this book and the most rewarding to get right.

The menu

  • Gougères · Soupe à l'oignon gratinée
  • Steak frites — entrecôte with bordelaise, filet with béarnaise (Chapters 51 and 52)
  • Frites · Salade verte with mustard vinaigrette
  • Tarte Tatin

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: briquettes for the cold, plus 2 oak chunks for the steak fire. Add 25 percent below 40°F.

Kettle A — steak and frites. Two-zone, built fierce. A Dutch oven of oil sits on the indirect side for the frites and comes over to direct heat to bring the temperature up between fries. Steaks sear last, after the second fry.

Kettle B — stone, then deflector. Gougères on the stone at 400°F first. Then the deflector at 350°F for the onion soup crocks to gratinée, and the tarte Tatin at the end.

Charcoal: about 3 chimneys on A, 2.5 on B.

Gougères
Appetizer · Makes 40 · 35 min

Choux pastry with cheese in it. They rise on steam alone, which means the fire has to be hot when they go in and the lid must stay down — one peek in the first fifteen minutes and they collapse.

  • 1 cup water · 1 stick butter · 1 tsp salt
  • 1¼ cups flour
  • 4 eggs, plus 1 for glazing
  • 5 oz Gruyère or Comté, finely grated, plus more for the tops
  • ½ tsp Dijon · pinch cayenne · black pepper
  1. Bring the water, butter and salt to a rolling boil in a pan on the grill. Tip in all the flour at once and beat hard with a wooden spoon until it forms a smooth ball that pulls from the sides — about 2 minutes. Keep beating over the heat for one more minute to dry it out.
  2. Cool for 5 minutes, then beat in the eggs one at a time, fully incorporating each. The paste will look broken after each addition and come back together; that is normal.
  3. Beat in the cheese, mustard, cayenne and pepper.
  4. Pipe or spoon walnut-sized mounds onto parchment, 2 inches apart. Flatten any peaks with a wet finger — peaks burn.
  5. Egg wash, scatter more cheese, and bake on the stone over a deflector at 400°F for 25 to 30 minutes, rotating once at the twenty-minute mark and not before.
  6. They should be deep gold, hollow-sounding and completely dry. Underbaked gougères deflate as they cool.
To the table · basketWarm, in a cloth-lined basket, with a glass of something cold in the other hand — gougères are a standing-up food eaten before anyone sits. Pile them loosely; they steam each other soft if packed tight.
Soupe à l'Oignon Gratinée
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 2 hr

The onions take an hour and there is no way around it. Anything less and you have onion-flavoured broth rather than onion soup.

  • 8 large onions, halved and sliced thin
  • 4 tbsp butter · 2 tbsp oil · 1 tsp salt · 1 tsp sugar
  • ½ cup dry white wine · 2 tbsp brandy
  • 8 cups beef stock (the fire-roasted one, Chapter 51)
  • 2 sprigs thyme · 1 bay leaf
  • 1 baguette, sliced and grilled
  • 10 oz Gruyère, grated
  1. Cook the onions in butter and oil in a Dutch oven over moderate heat with the salt and sugar, stirring every few minutes, for 50 to 70 minutes, until deep mahogany and jammy. Scrape the fond off the bottom each time it builds.
  2. Deglaze with brandy, then white wine, and reduce almost away.
  3. Add the stock, thyme and bay. Simmer 30 minutes on the indirect side.
  4. Ladle into heatproof crocks, float a grilled baguette slice on each, and bury it under grated Gruyère right to the rim — the cheese must touch the edges so it welds to the crock.
  5. Set the crocks on the indirect side, and shovel a few lit coals onto the deflector plate beside them or run the kettle at 450°F, lid down, 10 to 12 minutes, until the cheese is bubbling and blistered brown.
To the table · bowlIn the crock, on a plate, on a folded napkin because it will be too hot to hold. The cheese should have welded over the rim and drooped down the outside — that overspill is the mark of a properly gratinéed soup and scraping it off is vandalism.
Steak Frites, Two Ways
Main · Serves 8 · 25 min
  • 4 entrecôte / ribeye steaks, 12 oz each, 1¼ inches thick
  • 4 filet mignon, 7 oz each, barded with pancetta
  • Coarse salt and coarse black pepper
  • Bordelaise (Chapter 51) and béarnaise (Chapter 52)
  • Butter, thyme, garlic
  1. Dry-brine both cuts 24 hours ahead. Room temperature before cooking.
  2. Cook the entrecôte hot and fast, 2½ to 3 minutes a side to 128°F. Cook the filet as in Chapter 53 — seared all round, then indirect to 125°F.
  3. Rest both 8 minutes on a warm plate. Build the bordelaise in the resting pan while they sit.
  4. Slice the entrecôte across the grain; leave the filets whole.
  5. Serve each guest a choice, with frites, and the two sauces in separate jugs — the point of the menu is that people taste both against the same potato.
Frites
Side · Serves 8 · 40 min

Double-fried, as they must be. The first fry cooks the potato through; the second, much hotter, builds the shell. One fry gives you a limp chip and no amount of salt hides it.

  • 5 lb Maris Piper or Russet potatoes, cut into ⅜-inch batons
  • 2 qt frying oil — beef tallow if you want them properly good
  • Flaky salt
  1. Soak the cut batons in cold water for at least an hour, changing it twice, until the water runs clear. This removes surface starch, which otherwise burns.
  2. Drain and dry them completely. Wet potatoes in hot oil are dangerous as well as soggy.
  3. First fry at 300°F, in batches, 6 to 8 minutes — pale, soft, no colour at all. Drain on a rack.
  4. Let them cool at least 30 minutes. They can sit for hours at this stage, which is what makes this workable for a dinner party.
  5. Second fry at 375°F, in batches, 2 to 3 minutes, until deep gold and audibly crisp.
  6. Salt the instant they leave the oil, in a bowl, tossing. Salt applied later slides off.
To the table · basketPaper-lined basket or a metal cone, in a single loose heap, at the table within ninety seconds. Never on the plate under the steak, where the juices soften them. Mayonnaise or aioli on the side, and nobody should have to ask for more salt.
Salade Verte
Side · Serves 8 · 5 min
  • 2 heads butter lettuce, leaves whole
  • 1 tbsp Dijon · 2 tbsp red wine vinegar
  • 6 tbsp olive oil · 1 small shallot, minced
  • Salt, pepper · chives or chervil
  1. Whisk the mustard, vinegar, shallot and seasoning, then drizzle in the oil until it emulsifies and thickens.
  2. Dress the leaves at the table, seconds before eating — with your hands, lightly. The salad should look barely touched.
  3. This course comes after the steak, in the French order, and it is there to cut the butter. Do not add tomato, cucumber or anything else to it.
Tarte Tatin
Dessert · Serves 8 · 1 hr
  • 8 firm apples, peeled, halved and cored
  • 1 cup sugar · 1 stick butter · pinch of salt
  • 1 sheet all-butter puff pastry, cut to a circle slightly larger than the pan
  • Crème fraîche, to serve
  1. Melt the butter and sugar in a 10-inch cast-iron skillet over direct heat and cook to a deep amber caramel — darker than feels safe, because the apples will dilute it.
  2. Pack the apple halves in tightly, cut side up, standing on their edges in a spiral. They shrink considerably, so crowd them.
  3. Cook over moderate direct heat 20 minutes, basting with the caramel, until the apples soften and the caramel thickens.
  4. Lay the pastry over the top and tuck the edges down inside the pan, around the fruit.
  5. Move to the indirect side over a deflector at 400°F for 25 to 30 minutes, until the pastry is puffed and deep gold.
  6. Rest 10 minutes — no longer. Too soon and the caramel runs everywhere; too long and it welds to the pan.
  7. Put a plate over the skillet and invert in one confident movement, with gloves on.
To the table · plateInvert it at the table if your nerve holds — that flip is the moment. Serve warm, in wedges, with cold crème fraîche in a bowl and nothing else. Spoon any caramel left in the pan back over the top.
Kir & What to Pour
Drinks · Serves 8
  • Crème de cassis · dry white Burgundy or Aligoté (kir), or Crémant (kir royale)
  • Ratio: 1 part cassis to 9 parts wine
  1. Cassis in the bottom of the glass, wine poured over. Do not stir; it mixes on its own and the gradient looks better.
  2. With the steak: a Bordeaux or a Rhône with the entrecôte, a lighter Burgundy with the filet. Open both and let people compare — it is a two-sauce menu, so make it a two-wine one.
  3. Afterwards: Chartreuse or a marc, very cold, in small glasses.
  4. Non-alcoholic: cassis syrup with sparkling water in the same ratio, and a jug of citron pressé — lemon juice, sugar syrup and cold water served separately so people mix their own.

Grand Aïoli

Summer · Long lunch · One kettle · Embers

The Provençal Friday ritual: a mountain of vegetables, fish and eggs arranged around a mortar of garlic mayonnaise, eaten slowly over several hours in the shade. Almost nothing is cooked to order, most of it can be room temperature, and the aioli is the entire point — which makes it the least stressful menu in this book to host and the hardest to get exactly right.

The menu

  • Anchoïade and tapenade with raw vegetables
  • Aïoli — the centrepiece
  • Salt cod and grilled fish · Ember vegetables · Eggs, potatoes and beans
  • Grilled apricots with lavender honey

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: lump charcoal, plus vine cuttings or olive wood if you have them.

One kettle. Build a wide bed and work through it as the heat falls. Embers first for the whole vegetables — peppers, onions, fennel, aubergine buried in the coals. Then a direct grate for the fish, which takes eight minutes. Then the fading fire for the apricots. Potatoes, eggs and beans are boiled ahead and served at room temperature, which is what makes this work on a single grill.

Charcoal: about 2 chimneys total.

  • Fuel: lump ~12 lb · vine cuttings or olive wood
Aïoli
The centrepiece · Makes 3 cups · 20 min

Not garlic mayonnaise with a French name. Real aioli is garlic pounded to a paste with salt and emulsified with olive oil, and it should be so strong that a teaspoon makes you blink. Everything else on the table is a vehicle for it.

  • 12 cloves garlic, green germ removed
  • 1 tsp coarse salt
  • 2 egg yolks, at room temperature
  • 2 cups olive oil — half good fruity, half mild, or it turns bitter
  • Juice of ½ lemon · a little warm water
  1. Pound the garlic and salt in a mortar to a completely smooth, wet paste. This takes five minutes of work and there is no substitute — a blender bruises garlic and turns it acrid.
  2. Work in the yolks with the pestle until glossy.
  3. Add the oil drop by drop at first, turning the pestle constantly in one direction. Only once it has visibly thickened can you move to a thin stream. The first quarter-cup takes as long as the rest.
  4. If it stiffens to the point of cracking, loosen with a few drops of warm water and carry on.
  5. Finish with lemon. It should stand up in the mortar and hold the shape of the pestle.
  6. If it splits: start a fresh yolk in a clean bowl and whisk the broken aioli into it a drop at a time.
To the table · communalServe it in the mortar you made it in, pestle left beside it, in the middle of the table — this is a centrepiece, not a condiment, and decanting it into a ramekin misses the point entirely. Everything else radiates outward from it.
Salt Cod and Grilled Fish
Main · Serves 8 · 15 min plus soaking
  • 2 lb salt cod, soaked 48 hours with the water changed three times daily
  • 2 lb firm white fish fillets or 4 small whole fish
  • Olive oil · lemon · thyme · coarse salt
  • 1 bay leaf, 1 onion, peppercorns (for poaching)
  1. Taste a corner of the soaked cod before you cook it. If it is still aggressively salty, soak it longer — this is the only part of the dish that can go badly wrong.
  2. Poach the cod gently in water with the bay, onion and peppercorns for 8 to 10 minutes, until it flakes. Never boil it; it goes stringy.
  3. Grill the fresh fish over direct heat, oiled and salted, 3 to 4 minutes per side for fillets or 6 to 8 for small whole fish.
  4. Serve both at room temperature or barely warm, broken into large flakes, with lemon and oil.
Ember Vegetables
Main platter · Serves 8 · 45 min
  • 4 red peppers · 3 aubergines · 3 fennel bulbs, halved
  • 6 onions, unpeeled · 4 beetroots, wrapped in foil
  • 6 carrots
  • Olive oil, red wine vinegar, thyme, coarse salt
  1. Bury the peppers, aubergines, onions and foil-wrapped beetroots directly in the coals. Peppers 20 minutes, aubergines 25 to 35, onions 40, beets 50 to 60.
  2. Put each into a covered bowl for 10 minutes as it comes out, then peel. The skins slide off.
  3. Grill the fennel and carrots on the grate instead — they want colour rather than collapse.
  4. Tear everything into large pieces by hand and dress with oil, a splash of vinegar, thyme and salt. Serve at room temperature.
To the table · platterOne enormous platter, or a table with no plates on it at all — vegetables, fish, eggs, potatoes and beans arranged in loose groups by colour, radiating out from the mortar of aioli. Height and abundance are the whole aesthetic. Lemon halves and a salt dish tucked in among them.
Eggs, Potatoes and Beans
Main platter · Serves 8 · Make ahead
  • 8 eggs, boiled 8 minutes and halved
  • 3 lb small waxy potatoes, boiled in well-salted water, left whole
  • 1½ lb green beans, blanched 4 minutes and refreshed
  • 8 tomatoes, quartered · radishes, whole with tops
  • Olive oil · flaky salt
  1. Everything is cooked ahead and served at room temperature — which is what makes a grand aïoli feasible for eight on one kettle.
  2. Salt the potato water heavily; boiled potatoes seasoned afterwards never taste seasoned.
  3. Dress nothing except with oil and salt. The aioli is the sauce and anything else competes with it.
Tapenade & Anchoïade
Appetizer · Makes 2 cups each · 15 min
  • Tapenade
  • 2 cups black olives, pitted · 3 tbsp capers · 4 anchovy fillets
  • 1 clove garlic · 1 tsp thyme · ⅓ cup olive oil · lemon
  • Anchoïade
  • 1 tin (2 oz) anchovies · 3 cloves garlic · 2 tbsp red wine vinegar
  • ½ cup olive oil · black pepper
  1. Pound or pulse the tapenade to a coarse paste — it should look chopped, not smooth.
  2. Pound the anchoïade smoother, then work in the oil until it emulsifies into something spreadable.
  3. Serve with raw radishes, fennel and celery, and grilled bread, while the vegetables are still in the fire.
Grilled Apricots with Lavender Honey
Dessert · Serves 8 · 10 min
  • 16 apricots, halved and stoned
  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  • ⅓ cup lavender honey
  • 1½ cups fromage blanc or ricotta
  • ½ cup almonds, toasted and crushed · a few thyme sprigs
  1. Brush the cut faces with oil and grill cut side down over the fading coals, 3 to 4 minutes, until marked and just softened. Apricots collapse fast — pull them while they still hold.
  2. Spoon fromage blanc onto a platter, arrange the hot apricots over it, and pour the honey across while everything is still warm.
  3. Almonds and a scatter of thyme leaves. A few grains of sea salt on the honey.
Pastis & Rosé
Drinks · Serves 8
  • Pastis · very cold water · ice
  • Ratio: 1 part pastis to 5 parts water
  • Provençal rosé, thoroughly chilled
  • Non-alcoholic
  • Almond syrup (orgeat) and water · lemon and mint · sparkling water
  1. Pour the pastis first, then add water at the table so people watch it turn cloudy. Ice goes in last, never first — ice before water makes the anise crystallise and go grainy.
  2. Rosé all afternoon, in large glasses, refilled constantly. This is a three-hour lunch.
  3. For a non-alcoholic version of the same ritual, serve orgeat and water in the same way — cloudy, sweet, and poured at the table.
