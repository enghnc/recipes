Part Thirteen · Sandwiches

New York Deli

Winter · Two weeks of lead time · Two kettles · Deflector

Pastrami is the most involved thing in this book and the most worth doing: a brisket brined for a week, rubbed in coarse pepper and coriander, smoked for eight hours and then steamed for three more. That last step is the one everybody skips and the one that makes it pastrami rather than peppery brisket. Around it, rye bread off the stone, half-sour pickles you fermented yourself, latkes, and an egg cream.

The menu

  • Half-sour pickles · Matzo ball soup
  • Pastrami on deli rye, with mustard and nothing else
  • Latkes · Coleslaw and Russian dressing
  • Black-and-white cookies · Egg creams

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: briquettes for the long smoke, with oak and a little cherry. Pastrami wants less smoke than brisket — the pepper crust is doing most of the work.

Kettle A — the smoke, then the steam. Snake at 250°F for 6 to 8 hours to an internal 165°F. Then the same kettle converts: a deep pan of water on the grate, the pastrami on a rack above it, foil sealed over the top, lid down, 2 to 3 hours to 203°F.

Kettle B — rye, soup, latkes. Stone at 450°F for the rye. Deflector at 325°F for the matzo ball soup, which holds happily for hours. A Dutch oven of oil over direct heat for the latkes at the very end.

Charcoal: about 5 chimneys on A across the day, 3 on B.

Pastrami
Main · Serves 10–12 · 8 days curing, 11 hr cooking

Three distinct processes: cure, smoke, steam. Skipping the steam gives you smoked spiced brisket, which is a fine thing and is not pastrami — the steam is what makes the fat translucent and the slices yielding enough to fold.

  • 1 beef brisket flat, 6–7 lb, fat trimmed to ¼ inch
  • Brine · per gallon of water
  • 1 cup kosher salt · ½ cup brown sugar
  • 5 tsp pink curing salt #1
  • 3 tbsp pickling spice · 8 cloves garlic, crushed · 2 tbsp honey
  • Rub
  • ½ cup coarsely cracked black peppercorns
  • ¼ cup coarsely cracked coriander seed
  • 2 tbsp granulated garlic · 1 tbsp granulated onion · 1 tbsp brown sugar
  1. Brine, 7 to 8 days. Boil a quart of the water with all the brine ingredients to dissolve, then add the rest cold. Chill completely before the meat goes in — never brine in warm liquid.
  2. Submerge the brisket, weight it so it stays under, and refrigerate. Turn it once a day. For a flat thicker than 2 inches, inject a cup of brine into the centre on day one.
  3. Desalinate. Drain and soak in fresh cold water for 6 to 12 hours, changing the water twice. Fry a scrap and taste it — it should be seasoned, not salty. This step is adjustable and worth adjusting.
  4. Pat dry and coat heavily with the pepper-coriander rub, pressing it on. The crust should be thick enough to obscure the meat entirely; this is the defining characteristic.
  5. Smoke. Snake at 250°F with oak and one cherry chunk. 6 to 8 hours to 165°F internal.
  6. Steam. Set a deep pan of water on the grate, the pastrami on a rack above it, seal foil over the whole thing, and close the lid. 2 to 3 hours to 203°F, and the probe must slide in with no resistance at all.
  7. Rest 45 minutes. Slice by hand, against the grain, about ¼ inch thick — a machine slices it too thin and it falls apart.
  8. Pile it high on rye with brown mustard. Nothing else goes on a pastrami sandwich.
Curing salt

Pink curing salt #1 (6.25% sodium nitrite) is what makes cured meat safe over a week-long brine and what gives pastrami its pink colour. Measure it precisely — 5 teaspoons per gallon, no more — and never substitute Himalayan pink salt, which is an entirely different and useless thing. Keep it labelled and away from children. Without it, do not brine for more than two days.

Corned beef

Stop after step 3, simmer the desalinated brisket in fresh water with pickling spice for 3 hours until tender, and you have corned beef. Pastrami is corned beef that was then rubbed, smoked and steamed — which is the whole relationship between the two.

Mustard on the bread, never on the meat. A half-sour on the plate and nothing else — lettuce, tomato or mayonnaise on a pastrami sandwich is a fighting matter.
Half-Sour Pickles
Appetizer · Makes 3 lb · 3–5 days

Fermented in salt brine rather than pickled in vinegar, which is why they stay bright green and snappy. Half-sours are pulled young, while the inside is still translucent.

  • 3 lb small Kirby cucumbers, blossom ends trimmed off
  • 6 tbsp kosher salt dissolved in 2 qt filtered water
  • 1 head garlic, cloves smashed
  • 1 large bunch dill, ideally with flower heads
  • 2 tbsp pickling spice · 1 tsp black peppercorns · 1 tsp coriander
  • 1 grape or oak leaf (optional; the tannins keep them crisp)
  1. Trim the blossom end off every cucumber. It carries an enzyme that turns pickles soft, and this single step is the difference between crisp and mushy.
  2. Pack into a jar or crock with the garlic, dill and spices. Pour the brine over and weight everything below the surface.
  3. Leave at cool room temperature. It will go cloudy and start to bubble by day two — that is the fermentation working.
  4. Taste from day three. Half-sour at 3 to 5 days, when the centre is still pale green and crunchy; full-sour at two weeks.
  5. Refrigerate to stop them where you like them. They keep a month and slowly get more sour.
To the table · bowlWhole, wet, in a bowl with some of their brine and the dill still in it, on the table from the moment people arrive. Never sliced and never drained. One on every plate alongside the sandwich is not optional.
Matzo Ball Soup
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 3 hr
  • Stock
  • 1 whole chicken or 3 lb wings and backs
  • 3 onions, 4 carrots, 4 celery stalks · parsley stalks · 1 tbsp peppercorns · 2 bay leaves
  • Matzo balls
  • 1 cup matzo meal · 4 eggs
  • ¼ cup schmaltz or neutral oil · ¼ cup seltzer
  • 1 tsp salt · 1 tsp baking powder (for floaters) · pepper · dill
  1. Cover the chicken with cold water and bring very slowly to a bare shiver — never boil, or the stock clouds. Skim, add the aromatics, and simmer 2½ hours. Strain.
  2. Mix the matzo ball ingredients to a loose batter and refrigerate 1 hour. It firms up considerably and is unworkable straight away.
  3. Wet your hands and roll walnut-sized balls, handling them as little as possible — compressed matzo balls sink and stay dense.
  4. Simmer them in salted water, covered, 30 to 35 minutes. They double in size.
  5. Serve in hot stock with sliced carrot and a lot of dill.
To the table · bowlWide shallow bowls, one large ball per person sitting proud of the golden broth, carrot coins around it, dill scattered. Ladle the stock over the ball at the table so it does not soak and swell on the way.
Deli Rye
Bread · Makes 2 loaves · 4 hr
  • 2 cups rye flour · 3½ cups bread flour
  • 2¼ tsp instant yeast · 1 tbsp salt · 1 tbsp sugar
  • 2 tbsp caraway seed, plus more for the top
  • 1½ cups warm water · 2 tbsp oil · 1 tbsp molasses
  • 1 egg white beaten with 1 tbsp water · cornmeal for the peel
  1. Mix and knead 10 minutes. Rye is low in gluten, so the dough stays slightly tacky — resist adding flour.
  2. Rise 1½ hours, shape into two fat oval loaves, and proof 45 minutes on cornmeal.
  3. Slash three times across the top and brush with egg white. Scatter caraway.
  4. Stone at 450°F. Slide the loaves on and throw a cup of water onto the charcoal grate for steam.
  5. 30 to 35 minutes, until deep brown and 200°F inside.
  6. Cool completely. Slice thin — deli rye is cut thinner than sandwich bread so the meat dominates.
Latkes
Side · Makes 24 · 30 min
  • 5 lb russet potatoes, peeled
  • 2 onions
  • 2 eggs · ⅓ cup matzo meal or flour · 2 tsp salt · 1 tsp pepper
  • Frying oil, ½ inch deep
  • Sour cream and apple sauce, to serve
  1. Grate the potatoes and onions on the coarse side of a box grater — the onion also stops the potato browning.
  2. Wring them out hard in a towel and let the liquid stand for a minute. Pour the water off and scrape the white starch from the bottom of the bowl back into the potatoes. That starch is the binder.
  3. Mix with the eggs, matzo meal and seasoning. Fry immediately — the mixture greys within minutes.
  4. Fry in ½ inch of oil at 350°F, flattened thin, 3 to 4 minutes a side, until deep brown at the edges.
  5. Drain on a rack, salt at once, and keep warm in a single layer on the cool side of the grill.
To the table · platterSingle layer on a paper-lined platter, never stacked, with sour cream in one bowl and apple sauce in another so the argument can be had properly. Salt visible on top. They are perfect for ten minutes.
Coleslaw & Russian Dressing
Sides · Serves 8
  • 1 green cabbage and 4 carrots, shredded
  • ¾ cup mayonnaise · 2 tbsp cider vinegar · 1 tbsp sugar · 1 tsp celery seed
  • Russian dressing
  • 1 cup mayonnaise · ⅓ cup ketchup or chilli sauce
  • 2 tbsp prepared horseradish · 1 tbsp minced onion
  • 2 tbsp minced half-sour pickle · 1 tsp Worcestershire · paprika
  1. Dress the slaw at least 2 hours ahead so it softens slightly.
  2. Whisk the Russian dressing and rest it overnight. It belongs on a Rachel or a corned beef sandwich, never on the pastrami — that takes mustard alone.
Black-and-White Cookies
Dessert · Makes 16 · 40 min

Not really a cookie — a small flat cake, iced flat side up, with a hard line down the middle.

  • 1¾ cups flour · ½ tsp baking powder · ½ tsp salt
  • 1 stick butter · ¾ cup sugar · 1 egg
  • ½ cup buttermilk · 1 tsp vanilla · ½ tsp lemon zest
  • Icing
  • 3 cups powdered sugar · 3 tbsp corn syrup · 4–6 tbsp hot water
  • 3 tbsp cocoa powder, for half
  1. Cream the butter and sugar, beat in the egg, then alternate the dry ingredients and the buttermilk.
  2. Drop eight large spoonfuls per tray, well apart — they spread considerably.
  3. Bake over a deflector at 375°F for 14 to 16 minutes, until set and pale with just-golden edges. They should not brown.
  4. Cool completely, then turn them flat side up — that is the side you ice.
  5. Thin half the icing with hot water to a spreadable paste and ice one half of each cookie. Stir cocoa into the rest and do the other half, meeting in a clean straight line.
  6. Let the icing set hard, at least an hour.
Egg Cream
Drink · Per glass

No egg and no cream, and the order is not negotiable.

  • 2 tbsp chocolate syrup (Fox's U-bet, if you are being strict)
  • ¼ cup very cold whole milk
  • Cold seltzer, from a fresh bottle
  1. Syrup in the bottom of a tall chilled glass, then the milk.
  2. Hold a spoon against the inside of the glass and spray the seltzer hard against it so a white foam head builds two inches thick.
  3. Stir only the bottom, leaving the head intact. Drink immediately — it has about a minute.
  4. Also pour: Dr. Brown's Cel-Ray if you can get it, and cold lager for anyone who cannot face celery soda.
