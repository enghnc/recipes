Part Nine · Cook to Impress

The Showpieces

A showpiece is not a harder recipe. It is a recipe with a moment in it — and the whole skill is making sure people are watching when the moment arrives.

How to choreograph one

Every dish in this chapter has a single instant that carries it: the salt crust cracking, the steak coming off the coals, the marrow bones going down. Four rules make that instant land.

  • Do the reveal where people are. Not in the kitchen, not at the grill with your back turned. Carry it over first, then open it.
  • Announce it thirty seconds early. People need time to put down their drinks and turn round. A showpiece performed to an inattentive table is just food.
  • Only one per meal. Two theatrical moments cancel each other out, and the second always lands flatter than the first.
  • Practise the mechanics once. Not the cooking — the carrying, the cracking, the lighting. Every failure in this chapter is a handling failure, never a heat failure.

These are written as standalone centrepieces. Drop any one of them into an existing menu in place of its main course, keep that menu's sides and drinks, and the fuel plan barely changes.

Salt-Dome Whole Fish
Showpiece · Serves 6–8 · 45 min · Deflector

A whole fish buried in a salt-and-egg-white crust that sets into a hard shell, steams the fish in its own moisture, and comes off in slabs when you crack it. The fish is not salty — the crust never touches the flesh, only the skin, which you peel away with it.

  • 1 whole fish, 4–5 lb — sea bass, snapper, branzino or turbot, gutted but not scaled
  • 6 lb coarse sea salt (yes, really)
  • 5 egg whites
  • ½ cup water
  • 2 lemons, sliced · 1 bunch fennel fronds or dill · 4 sprigs thyme
  • Olive oil and lemon, to finish
  1. Leave the scales on. They are the barrier between the salt and the flesh. A scaled fish under a salt crust becomes inedible.
  2. Stuff the cavity with lemon, herbs and thyme.
  3. Beat the egg whites to loose foam and fold through the salt with the water until it feels like wet sand that holds a shape when squeezed.
  4. Spread a ½-inch bed of the mixture on a foil tray, lay the fish on it, and pack the rest over the top, sealing all the way to the tray. Leave the tail poking out as a handle and a doneness gauge.
  5. Set on the indirect side over a deflector at 400°F. Cook 25 to 35 minutes — roughly 12 minutes per pound. The crust will turn pale gold and crack slightly.
  6. Probe through the crust at the thickest point: 135°F. Rest 10 minutes; it carries on cooking inside the shell.
  7. The moment: carry the tray to the table, tap once firmly along the spine with the back of a heavy knife, then lever the crust up in two or three large slabs. Steam and herbs come out with it.
  8. Peel the skin away with the crust, lift the top fillets off the bone, then lift the whole spine out in one piece and serve the bottom fillets. Dress with oil and lemon only.
Watch out

Brush every loose salt crystal off the fillets before serving. A crumb of crust that falls onto the flesh is unpleasantly salty and will be the one thing anyone remembers.

To the table · boardThe sealed dome comes over first, entirely unopened and looking like nothing — half the effect is that people cannot tell what it is. Crack it in front of them, lift the slabs off onto a second tray, and serve the fillets from the broken shell itself. Oil, lemon and flaky salt on the table; nothing else.
Caveman Ribeye steak cooked directly on the coals
Showpiece · Serves 4–6 · 20 min · No grate

The grate comes off and the steak goes straight onto burning charcoal. It sounds like a stunt and it is a genuinely superior method — direct contact with the coals gives a crust no grate can match, and because there is no air gap the interior cooks less than you expect. Almost no ash sticks; what does, you brush off.

  • 2 bone-in ribeyes, 1½–2 inches thick, about 1½ lb each
  • Coarse salt and coarse black pepper
  • 3 tbsp butter · 2 sprigs thyme · 1 clove garlic
  • Hardwood lump charcoal only — never briquettes
  1. Lump charcoal is not optional. Briquettes contain binders and fillers you do not want touching food directly, and they ash far more heavily.
  2. Salt the steaks 45 minutes ahead and leave them uncovered at room temperature. Dry surface, no oil — oil would flare.
  3. Light two full chimneys and spread them in an even, deep bed. Wait until every coal is ashed grey and glowing, with no visible flame.
  4. Fan or blow the loose ash off the surface of the bed — a hairdryer or a sheet of cardboard works. This is what keeps the steaks clean.
  5. Lay the steaks directly onto the coals. They will hiss violently. Leave them 4 to 6 minutes without touching.
  6. Flip with tongs onto a fresh part of the bed. Another 4 to 6 minutes. Pull at 120–125°F — carryover is significant here.
  7. Brush off any clinging embers with a pastry brush, rest 10 minutes with butter, thyme and a crushed garlic clove melting on top.
  8. Slice off the bone, then across the grain, and finish with flaky salt.
Why it works

On a grate, the steak is heated by radiation across an air gap and only the bars touch it. On the coals, conduction happens across the entire surface at once, so the crust forms faster and the heat has less time to travel inward. The result is a thicker crust and a wider band of rare.

To the table · boardLet people watch you lay them on the coals — that is the moment, not the serving. Rest and slice on a board where they can see the cross-section, fan the slices back against the bone, and put the bone on the board too. Flaky salt in a dish. No sauce, or one of the compound butters passed separately.
Reverse-Seared Tomahawk
Showpiece · Serves 4–6 · 1½ hr · Deflector

The most photographed steak there is, and the one most often ruined by cooking it like a thin steak. A 2½-inch cut needs to come up slowly and sear last, or you get a grey band an inch thick around a cold centre.

  • 1 tomahawk ribeye, 2½ inches thick, 2½–3 lb
  • 1 tbsp coarse salt · 1 tbsp coarse black pepper
  • 2 tbsp beef tallow or neutral oil
  • Maître d'hôtel or café de Paris butter, to finish
  1. Dry-brine 24 hours ahead: salt all over, uncovered, on a rack in the fridge.
  2. Deflector in, kettle at 225°F. Steak on the indirect side with a probe in the centre.
  3. Bring it up slowly to 115°F — 50 to 75 minutes. Do not rush this and do not open the lid to look.
  4. Pull the steak and rest it 10 minutes while you remove the deflector and build a ferocious bed of coals directly under the grate.
  5. Oil the steak, not the grate. Sear 60 to 90 seconds per side, standing it on its fat edge with tongs for a final 30 seconds.
  6. Pull at 128–130°F. Rest 8 minutes with a slice of compound butter melting on top.
  7. Carve the eye off the bone in one piece, slice it across the grain, and return the slices to the bone to serve.
To the table · boardThe bone is the whole point of a tomahawk, so never lose it. Carve the eye off, slice it, and reassemble the slices along the bone so it reads as one piece again. Butter melting on top, flaky salt, a grilled lemon or a bowl of sauce beside. Bring a knife and let someone else take the first slice.
Fire-Roasted Bone Marrow
Showpiece · Serves 8 as a starter · 20 min

The best value showpiece in this chapter — marrow bones cost almost nothing and look extraordinary. Ask the butcher for centre-cut canoes, split lengthwise, not the cross-cut rounds.

  • 8 beef marrow bones, split lengthwise (canoe cut)
  • Coarse salt and coarse black pepper
  • Parsley salad
  • 1 large bunch flat-leaf parsley, leaves picked
  • 2 shallots, sliced paper-thin · 2 tbsp capers
  • Juice of 1 lemon · 2 tbsp olive oil · flaky salt
  • To serve
  • Grilled sourdough · flaky salt · long narrow spoons
  1. Soak the bones in salted cold water for 12 to 24 hours, changing the water twice, to draw out the blood. This is what makes marrow pale and clean rather than grey and metallic.
  2. Pat dry and season the cut face generously.
  3. Set them cut face up on the indirect side at 400°F. 12 to 18 minutes.
  4. They are ready when the marrow is soft, glossy and quivering but still holding its shape. Thirty seconds too long and it liquefies and runs out of the bone — watch them continuously from minute ten.
  5. Give them 60 seconds directly over the coals at the end for a blistered top, if you want the drama.
  6. Dress the parsley salad at the last second and pile it on a plate beside the bones.
  7. Serve with grilled bread, flaky salt, and a spoon per person. Scrape, spread, salt, top with parsley.
To the table · boardBones lined up in a row on a board, cut side up, with the marrow still trembling — get them there inside two minutes. Parsley salad in a heap at one end, grilled bread at the other, flaky salt in a small dish, and a narrow spoon laid across each bone. People assemble their own. Put an empty bowl out for the bones.
Paella over Live Fire
Showpiece · Serves 8 · 1 hr · Direct, wide bed

Paella is a fire dish that most people cook on a hob, which is why most paella is disappointing. The pan is wide and shallow so the liquid evaporates fast and the rice at the bottom toasts into socarrat — and only a wide fire heats a wide pan evenly.

  • 1 paella pan, 15–17 inches (fits a 22-inch kettle with the grate at the top setting)
  • 3 cups bomba or Calasparra rice
  • 8 cups hot stock — chicken, or shellfish shells simmered 30 min
  • Large pinch saffron, toasted and crushed
  • 1½ lb chicken thigh, in pieces · 8 oz cooking chorizo, sliced
  • 1 lb shrimp, shell on · 1 lb mussels or clams
  • 1 onion, 4 cloves garlic, 1 red pepper, all finely diced
  • 3 tomatoes, grated · 2 tsp sweet pimentón
  • 1 cup green beans or flat beans · ½ cup olive oil
  • Lemon wedges, to serve
  1. Build a wide, even bed of coals across the whole charcoal grate — wider than the pan. Uneven heat is the one thing that ruins paella and a banked fire will not work.
  2. Brown the chicken and chorizo in the oil, pushing them to the rim as they colour. Cook the onion, pepper and garlic in the fat, then the grated tomato and pimentón until dark and jammy — this sofrito is the flavour base and hurrying it is the most common mistake.
  3. Add the rice and stir for 2 minutes to coat every grain.
  4. Pour in the hot stock and the saffron. Spread everything into an even layer and do not stir again from this point. Stirring makes risotto; paella is not risotto.
  5. Boil hard for 10 minutes, then let the fire fall and simmer 10 minutes more. Push the shellfish into the surface in the last 8 minutes, hinge down.
  6. The socarrat: when the liquid is gone, put the pan back over the hottest part for 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Listen — you want crackling, not silence, and you should smell toast a moment before it smells burnt. That gap is about twenty seconds wide.
  7. Off the heat, cover with a cloth and rest 5 minutes. Lemon wedges around the rim.
To the table · in the panThe pan goes to the middle of the table on two trivets and never gets served out. Everyone eats from the wedge of pan directly in front of them, working inward — that is the Valencian convention and it is also how the socarrat gets distributed fairly. Lemon around the rim, a spoon each, no plates.
Côte de Boeuf for the Table
Showpiece · Serves 6 · 50 min · Two-zone

A double-bone rib roast cooked as a steak. It looks like a roast, carves like a steak, and feeds six from one piece of meat — which is a better trick than any single steak can manage.

  • 1 double-bone côte de boeuf, 4–4½ lb
  • 1½ tbsp coarse salt · 1 tbsp coarse pepper
  • 4 sprigs rosemary · 1 head garlic, halved across
  • 4 tbsp butter
  • Coarse salt and a sauce of choice, to serve
  1. Dry-brine 24 hours uncovered in the fridge. Bring fully to room temperature — a cold centre in a piece this thick is unrecoverable.
  2. Two-zone fire, hot side built with oak. Sear all over on the hot side, 3 minutes per face including the fat cap, about 12 minutes total.
  3. Move to the indirect side with the rosemary and halved garlic under it. Lid on.
  4. 25 to 35 minutes to 125°F, turning once.
  5. Rest 15 minutes with butter melting over, loosely tented.
  6. Carve the eye off the bones in one piece, then slice thickly across the grain. Chop the bones apart and put them on the board — someone always wants one.
To the table · boardBring it out whole and uncarved, rested, with the rosemary and blackened garlic still around it, and carve at the table. The slicing is the performance. Fan the slices, lean the bones against them, flaky salt in a dish, sauce passed separately in a warm jug.
Whole Turbot on the Bone
Showpiece · Serves 6 · 30 min · Direct, moderate

The most expensive thing in this book and the easiest to cook. Turbot is a flat, thick, gelatinous fish that is almost impossible to dry out, and a whole one on a platter is as impressive as a rib of beef.

  • 1 whole turbot, 4–5 lb (brill or a large plaice substitute)
  • Olive oil, coarse salt
  • Beurre noisette
  • 1 stick butter · 2 tbsp capers · juice of 1 lemon · parsley
  1. Score the dark skin three times down to the bone. Oil generously, salt heavily.
  2. Grill over moderate direct heat — not fierce — dark side down first, 10 to 12 minutes, using two spatulas to move it.
  3. Flip carefully and give the white side 8 to 10 minutes.
  4. Done at 135°F at the thickest point, or when the top fillets lift cleanly from the frame with a spoon.
  5. Brown the butter in a pan on the grill until it smells nutty, then add capers, lemon and parsley off the heat.
  6. Serve whole, and lift the fillets at the table — four from each fish, two per side.
To the table · platterWhole, dark side up, on the largest platter you own, with the beurre noisette poured over at the table so the capers scatter across the skin. Lift the fillets in front of people with a large spoon rather than a knife — turbot separates along its own lines and never needs cutting. The frame stays on the platter throughout.
Dry-Aged Ribeye at Home
Showpiece · 28–45 days ahead · Serves 4–6

A project rather than a recipe. Aging concentrates flavour by evaporating water and lets enzymes break down muscle fibre; the funk people associate with it arrives after about 45 days. You will lose 15 to 30 percent of the weight to trim and evaporation, which is why it costs what it costs in a restaurant.

  • 1 bone-in ribeye subprimal, 5–8 lb, fat cap intact
  • A dedicated fridge or a shelf with nothing aromatic in it
  • A wire rack and a small fan
  1. Only age a large, fat-capped, bone-in piece. Individual steaks have too much exposed surface and simply turn to leather.
  2. Pat dry and set on a wire rack over a tray, fat cap up, uncovered, in a fridge at 34–38°F with a small fan for air movement.
  3. Leave it alone. 28 days for concentration and tenderness; 45 for real blue-cheese funk. Turn it once a week.
  4. The surface will go dark, hard and unappetising — that is the pellicle and it is correct. It should smell nutty. If it smells sour, ammoniac or sweet, throw it away without debate.
  5. Trim the hardened exterior off with a sharp knife until you reach red meat, then cut into 1½-inch steaks.
  6. Cook as the caveman ribeye or the reverse-sear above. Season with salt only — 40 days of work should not meet a rub.
Food safety

This works because a dry, cold, moving-air surface is hostile to spoilage bacteria. Break any of those three conditions — wrap it, let the fridge warm, or stop the airflow — and you are no longer aging meat, you are spoiling it. When in doubt, discard.

To the table · boardShow people the trimmed-off crust before you cook — the dark rind next to the ruby interior is more impressive than the finished steak. Then serve it sliced, on a bare board, with nothing but flaky salt.
Asado al Palo lamb on a cross
Showpiece · Serves 12–20 · 4–6 hr · Beyond two kettles

Included honestly: this one does not fit the rules of this book. A whole lamb splayed on an iron cross beside an open fire needs ground, a fire pit and a licence to make a mess. It is here because it is the ancestor of every other recipe in this collection and because once in your life you should do it.

  • 1 whole lamb or kid, 20–30 lb, butterflied flat
  • 1 iron cross (estaca or asador), driven into the ground
  • Hardwood — oak, quebracho or fruitwood — burned down to embers in a separate fire
  • 2 cups coarse salt dissolved in 4 cups water, as salmuera
  • A branch of rosemary, as the brush
  1. Wire the butterflied lamb flat onto the cross, ribs facing out.
  2. Build a fire beside it, not under it, and rake embers toward the base as they form. The cross leans over the embers at roughly 20 degrees.
  3. Ribs toward the fire for the first two-thirds — about 3 hours — so the cavity renders slowly and bastes the meat.
  4. Turn the cross to face the skin side for the last 1 to 2 hours to crisp it.
  5. Brush with salmuera using the rosemary branch every 30 minutes, for seasoning and moisture both.
  6. Done at 195°F in the shoulder, when the meat gives at the bone. Total 4 to 6 hours depending on the wind — and the wind matters more than the clock.
  7. Lift the cross out, lay it flat on a table, and cut portions off it where it lies.
Scaled down

A butterflied lamb shoulder or a whole goat leg on a smaller vertical stake, leaning beside a two-zone kettle with the lid off, gets you a recognisable version for eight in about 3 hours. It is not the same thing, but it is the same idea.

To the table · communalThe lamb never leaves the cross. Lay it flat across a table or two trestles, hand out knives, and let people cut what they want from it. Chimichurri and salmuera in bowls, bread in baskets, and nothing else — a whole animal on a cross does not need a side dish.
