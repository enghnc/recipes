Part Eight · To the Table

Board, Platter, Plate

Plating is not decoration. It is the last cooking decision you make, and for grilled food it is mostly a decision about heat, crispness, and who does the assembling.

Restaurant plating solves a restaurant's problem: one portion, composed out of sight, carried twenty feet, eaten by one person who will never see the pan. Almost nothing in this book has that problem. Fire food is cooked in front of people, in quantities meant to be shared, and it is usually at its best within four minutes of leaving the grate. The plating question here is therefore not how do I make this look composed but what gets it to the table hottest, crispest, and with the least interference.

The four vessels

Nearly every dish in this book belongs in one of four places, and choosing correctly settles most of the other questions automatically.

VesselRight forBecause
BoardWhole roasts, sliced beef, chopped pork, anything carved at the tableWood holds heat gently, absorbs nothing, and signals that the food is communal. Juices stay where they fall and get mopped with bread.
PlatterSkewers, whole fish, quarters of chicken, sausages, vegetablesA flat surface keeps things in a single layer, which is the only way crisp stays crisp. Height and arrangement are visible.
BowlBeans, dal, stews, salads, anything with liquid or dressingDepth pools sauce where it belongs and lets you mound rather than spread.

The things that must not be plated

A meaningful share of this book is deliberately unplated, and imposing composition on it is a mistake rather than an improvement.

  • Assembly dishes. Tacos, bún chả, souvlaki, jerk sandwiches, lettuce wraps. The eater builds each mouthful, and the cook's job ends at putting hot components within reach. Building them for people removes the part they enjoy.
  • Handed food. Choripán, elote, festival, tarte flambée. These go from the fire to a hand to a mouth, often standing up, often with a napkin as the only crockery.
  • Table events. The clambake tipped onto newspaper. A lamb shoulder pulled apart by hand. Calçots unwrapped from their steaming bundle. The unveiling is the presentation, and doing it in the kitchen throws it away.
  • Anything with a two-minute window. Provoleta sets. Naan stiffens. Picarones go leathery. Speed beats arrangement every time.

Sauce: under, beside, or passed

Three rules cover almost every case.

Under, for anything with a crisp or lacquered exterior. Yogurt goes on the plate and the hot fruit sits in it; aji verde goes beside the chicken, never over it. Sauce poured over crackling, tandoori char or fish skin destroys in two seconds the thing that took three hours.

Beside, for anything where the sauce is one option among several. Chimichurri and salsa criolla sit in bowls; the asador does not dress anyone's meat.

Passed in a jug, for emulsions and pan sauces that people may want more of — Riesling sauce, ladolemono, lingonberry. Warm the jug. A cold jug drops a butter sauce below emulsion temperature before it reaches the far end of the table.

The one exception worth naming: a few sauces are supposed to drown the food. Mutton dip, nước chấm and mojo are not condiments — the meat sits in them, and holding back is the error.

Temperature is a plating decision

Warm your plates and platters for anything roasted, and chill them for anything raw or dressed. A cold platter under grilled lamb costs more heat than an extra five minutes of resting does, and it is the most common reason food arrives lukewarm at an outdoor table. The easiest method is the grill itself: stack plates on the indirect side with the lid down for ten minutes, or fill them with hot water and dry them as the meat rests.

Equally, several things in this book are explicitly not meant to be hot. Fagioli all'uccelletto, escalivada, zaalouk and the Moroccan salads are all served at room temperature, where the olive oil actually tastes of something. Serving them hot is as much a mistake as serving steak cold.

Garnish from the same fire

The best finishing touches in live-fire cooking are things the fire already made: a shard of crackling broken over the slices, charred lemon halves, a spoonful of drippings, blackened scallion, a scrap of burnt pineapple, the smoked salt in the bottom of the pan. Parsley chopped in the kitchen looks like an apology. A grilled lemon half looks like the rest of the meal.

Three habits worth keeping: scatter powders — chaat masala, Tajín, shichimi — from a height so they land evenly rather than in a stripe; add anything crisp last, after the sauce; and leave herbs in whole sprigs or torn leaves rather than chopping them, which is both faster and more honest about where the food comes from.

Height, negative space, and the one-layer rule

Grilled food looks best piled loosely and badly when spread flat and evenly. Pile skewers parallel and slightly overlapping; mound kraut down the centre of a platter rather than spreading it to the rim; stand ribs up rather than laying them down. Leave the outer inch of any platter empty — food running to the edge reads as crowded no matter how good it is.

The exception, and it overrides everything above: anything crisp goes in a single layer. Festival, tostones, wings, papad, crispbread. Stacking them traps steam and undoes the texture within minutes. If you cannot fit them in one layer, use two vessels.

The order things go down

Put the room-temperature dishes on the table first — salads, pickles, sauces, bread — then bring the hot things out last and all at once. It is the opposite of restaurant service and it is right for this food: the cook stays at the fire until the final minute, and nothing hot sits waiting for something cold to be fetched.

One board, used all evening

For any menu that runs in rounds — tarte flambée, yakitori, calçots, bún chả — designate a single board or platter that goes back and forth to the fire and gets reloaded. Wiping and reusing one vessel keeps the rhythm going. Bringing out a fresh clean plate for every round slows the whole thing to a halt and makes a casual meal feel like service.
