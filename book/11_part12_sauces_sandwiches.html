Part Twelve · The Barbecue Sauce Atlas

Ten Sauces, Eight Traditions

American barbecue sauce is not one thing that varies. It is several unrelated condiments that share a name, and the differences map almost exactly onto which animal a region cooks.

The map

The logic is consistent once you see it. Where the meat is whole hog or shoulder, the sauce is thin and sour — vinegar cuts fat and penetrates chopped meat. Where the meat is beef, the sauce is thin and savoury or absent entirely, because good brisket does not need help. Sweet, thick, tomato-heavy sauce belongs to ribs, where it can be lacquered on at the end without burning.

When sauce goes on

Thick, sugary sauces — Kansas City, Georgia peach — go on in the last twenty minutes and nowhere earlier, in two or three thin coats that each set before the next. Thin vinegar sauces go on as a mop, hourly, from hour three, and again at the table. Alabama white and mustard sauce are finishing sauces only — they are dressings, and heating them splits the first and dulls the second.

Kansas City Sauce
Makes 3 cups · 40 min

The sauce most people mean when they say barbecue sauce. The long slow simmer is what takes it from "ketchup with spices in it" to something with real depth.

  • 2 cups ketchup · ⅓ cup molasses · ⅓ cup dark brown sugar
  • ⅓ cup cider vinegar · 2 tbsp Worcestershire
  • 2 tbsp yellow mustard · 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • 1 tbsp smoked paprika · 2 tsp granulated onion · 2 tsp granulated garlic
  • 1 tsp black pepper · ½ tsp cayenne · ½ tsp ground allspice
  • ½ cup water
  1. Whisk everything together in a saucepan on the indirect side of the grill.
  2. Simmer 30 to 40 minutes, stirring every five, until it thickens enough to coat a spoon and the raw tomato edge has gone.
  3. It will thicken further as it cools. Thin with water if it gets past pourable.
  4. Rest overnight. Keeps three weeks refrigerated.
To the table · bowlWarmed, in a jug or squeeze bottle, always beside the meat and never on it — half of any table will not want it. The exception is ribs, where two thin coats set on the grill in the last twenty minutes are part of the cooking.
Memphis Sauce
Makes 3 cups · 30 min
  • 1½ cups ketchup · ¾ cup cider vinegar
  • ¼ cup brown sugar · 2 tbsp molasses
  • 2 tbsp yellow mustard · 1 tbsp Worcestershire
  • 1 tbsp paprika · 2 tsp black pepper · 1 tsp celery salt · ½ tsp cayenne
  • ½ cup water
  1. Simmer 20 to 25 minutes. It should stay noticeably thinner and sharper than Kansas City — closer to a dressing than a glaze.
  2. In Memphis, ribs are most often served dry, with rub only, and this arrives on the side for anyone who wants it. Serving it that way is more authentic than saucing the rack.
St. Louis Sauce
Makes 2½ cups · 20 min
  • 1½ cups ketchup · ¾ cup cider vinegar · ½ cup water
  • 3 tbsp brown sugar · 2 tbsp corn syrup
  • 1 tbsp yellow mustard · 1 tbsp Worcestershire
  • 1 tsp granulated onion · 1 tsp black pepper · ½ tsp cayenne · 1 tsp liquid smoke (optional)
  1. Simmer 15 to 20 minutes. This is the thinnest of the tomato sauces — pourable, sharp, and designed to be brushed onto grilled pork steaks repeatedly rather than glazed once.
  2. Keep it warm at the edge of the fire and mop it on through the last fifteen minutes of cooking.
Texas Mop
Makes 4 cups · 15 min

Barely a sauce. It exists to keep the surface of a brisket moist and to add a little acid and pepper as it cooks — Central Texas serves brisket naked, and anything thicker than this would be considered an insult to the meat.

  • 2 cups beef stock · 1 cup cider vinegar
  • ½ cup beef drippings or rendered fat (from the brisket itself, ideally)
  • ¼ cup Worcestershire · 2 tbsp coarse black pepper
  • 1 tbsp salt · 1 tbsp granulated garlic · 1 tsp cayenne
  • 1 onion, halved · 2 bay leaves
  1. Simmer everything for 15 minutes in a pot at the edge of the fire.
  2. Keep it warm for the whole cook and mop it on with a cotton mop or a thick brush every hour from hour three.
  3. Discard the onion and bay before serving what is left as a thin jus alongside.
Eastern North Carolina Sauce
Makes 4 cups · 5 min plus resting

Four ingredients, no cooking, and no tomato — the presence of tomato is the thing eastern Carolina defines itself against. It looks like almost nothing in the bowl and it transforms chopped pork completely.

  • 3 cups cider vinegar · 1 cup water
  • 2 tbsp red pepper flakes
  • 1 tbsp coarse black pepper · 1 tbsp salt
  • 2 tbsp sugar (optional, and contested)
  1. Stir together cold. Do not cook it.
  2. Rest at least 24 hours — the flakes need time to bloom and the raw vinegar edge needs to soften. Straight away it tastes like vinegar; the next day it tastes like barbecue.
  3. Use it two ways: tossed through the chopped pork the moment it comes off the board, and again in a bottle on the table. The tossing is not optional — this is the sauce that seasons the meat, not a condiment.
  4. Keeps indefinitely.
To the table · bowlIn a bottle or a jug with a narrow pour, because people will douse rather than spoon. It should look alarmingly thin and slightly pink with flakes floating in it. Anyone who has not met it before should be told to use more than feels sensible.
Lexington Dip Piedmont-style
Makes 3 cups · 10 min

Fifty miles west of eastern Carolina, a spoonful of ketchup goes in and a rivalry begins. It is still a thin vinegar sauce, but the faint sweetness makes it a different thing on the tongue and it is what dresses red slaw.

  • 2 cups cider vinegar · ¾ cup water
  • ⅓ cup ketchup · 3 tbsp brown sugar
  • 1 tbsp red pepper flakes · 2 tsp black pepper · 1 tbsp salt
  • 1 tsp hot sauce
  1. Warm gently for 5 minutes, just enough to dissolve the sugar. Do not reduce it.
  2. Rest overnight. Keeps a month.
  3. Use a cup of it to dress red slaw, toss some through the chopped pork, and put the rest on the table.
South Carolina Mustard Sauce Carolina gold
Makes 3 cups · 20 min

The German settlers of the South Carolina midlands brought mustard, and the sauce stayed. It is the best barbecue sauce for pork that most people have never tried.

  • 1½ cups yellow mustard
  • ½ cup cider vinegar · ⅓ cup honey · ¼ cup brown sugar
  • 2 tbsp ketchup · 1 tbsp Worcestershire
  • 1 tbsp hot sauce · 2 tsp black pepper · 1 tsp cayenne · 1 tsp granulated garlic
  • 2 tbsp butter
  1. Whisk everything but the butter and simmer very gently for 15 minutes — boiled mustard turns bitter, so keep it below a simmer if you can.
  2. Whisk in the butter off the heat for gloss.
  3. Rest overnight. It is sharp on day one and rounded on day two.
  4. Brush on chicken or pork in the last 10 minutes, and serve the rest alongside.
Alabama White Sauce
Makes 2½ cups · 5 min, no cook

Mayonnaise-based, invented in Decatur, and used as a dip for smoked chicken — traditionally the whole bird is dunked in it as it comes off the pit. It sounds wrong and converts everyone who tries it.

  • 1½ cups mayonnaise
  • ½ cup cider vinegar · 2 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1 tbsp prepared horseradish · 1 tbsp coarse black pepper
  • 1 tbsp sugar · 2 tsp granulated garlic · 1 tsp salt · ½ tsp cayenne
  1. Whisk together and refrigerate at least 4 hours, ideally overnight.
  2. Never heat it. It will split, and it cannot be brought back.
  3. Use it as a finishing dip for smoked or grilled chicken, brushed on in the final minute off the heat, or served as a dressing.
  4. Keeps a week. It thickens in the fridge; loosen with a splash of vinegar.
Georgia Peach Sauce
Makes 3 cups · 35 min
  • 4 ripe peaches, peeled and chopped (grill them first for depth)
  • 1 cup ketchup · ⅓ cup cider vinegar
  • ⅓ cup brown sugar · 3 tbsp bourbon
  • 1 tbsp Dijon · 1 tsp smoked paprika · ½ tsp cayenne · ½ tsp ginger
  1. Grill the peach halves cut side down for 4 minutes before chopping — the char is what keeps this from tasting like jam.
  2. Simmer everything 25 to 30 minutes until the fruit collapses.
  3. Blend smooth, or leave it rustic. Very high in sugar, so glaze only in the last 15 minutes.
Florida Citrus-Mango Sauce
Makes 3 cups · 25 min
  • 2 ripe mangoes, chopped · ½ cup orange juice
  • ¼ cup lime juice · ¼ cup cider vinegar
  • ⅓ cup brown sugar · 2 tbsp Dijon
  • 1 Scotch bonnet or habanero, seeded · 1 tsp allspice · 1 tsp salt
  1. Simmer 20 minutes, then blend smooth.
  2. Thin with orange juice to a pourable consistency — this one is meant to be loose.
  3. Excellent on jerk chicken, grilled fish and anything from the Caribbean chapters.

Carolina Pulled Pork

Late summer · Crowd · Two kettles · Deflector

The sandwich this book has been building toward since Chapter 14. One shoulder, twelve hours, and a flight of four sauces so people can settle the Carolina argument at your table — eastern, Lexington, South Carolina mustard and Alabama white, side by side with the same pork. Red slaw and white slaw, hush puppies, and banana pudding.

The menu

  • Boiled peanuts · Hush puppies
  • Pulled pork with the four-sauce flight (Chapter 56)
  • Red slaw and white slaw · Collard greens · soft buns
  • Banana pudding

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: briquettes for the snake, hickory and a little oak. Carolina pork takes a heavier smoke than most pork.

Kettle A — the shoulder. Snake at 250–275°F, water pan, hickory on the first third. An 8 lb bone-in Boston butt takes 10 to 12 hours to 203°F. Mop with eastern sauce hourly from hour four. No wrap if you want real bark; wrap at 165°F if you are short of time.

Kettle B — greens, hush puppies, peanuts. Deflector at 325°F for the collards, which run three hours. Boiled peanuts go on in the morning and simmer all day. A Dutch oven of oil comes over the direct side for the hush puppies at the end.

Charcoal: about 5 chimneys on A across the day, 3 on B.

  • Pantry: 2 lb raw green peanuts · cider vinegar (a large bottle — this menu drinks it) · red pepper flakes · ketchup · yellow mustard · honey · brown sugar · Worcestershire · mayonnaise · horseradish · cornmeal · flour · baking powder · buttermilk · frying oil · vanilla wafers · sweetened condensed milk · cornstarch · 16 soft buns
  • Dairy/Eggs: buttermilk · whole milk · 6 eggs · butter
  • Fuel: briquettes ~40 lb · hickory ×5 · oak ×2
Boiled Peanuts
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 6 hr, unattended

Raw green peanuts simmered in heavily salted water until soft — a Southern roadside staple that confuses everyone north of Virginia and then converts them. They cost almost nothing and they occupy people for hours.

  • 2 lb raw green peanuts, in the shell
  • 1 cup salt · 4 qt water
  • Optional: 2 tbsp Old Bay, or 3 dried chillies
  1. Rinse the peanuts and pick out any that float.
  2. Cover with the water and salt in a large pot on the indirect side of Kettle B. They must stay submerged — weight them with a plate.
  3. Simmer 4 to 7 hours, topping up water, until the kernels are as soft as a cooked bean. Start tasting at four hours.
  4. Let them cool in the brine for at least an hour. That is where most of the seasoning happens.
To the table · bowlWarm, wet, in a big bowl with a slotted spoon, with a second bowl for shells and a stack of paper towels. Eaten with fingers, standing up, hours before dinner. Serve them in their brine, not drained.
Pulled Pork
Main · Serves 12–16 · 12 hr plus rest
  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder (Boston butt), 8–9 lb
  • 3 tbsp coarse salt · 2 tbsp coarse black pepper
  • 2 tbsp paprika · 1 tbsp brown sugar · 1 tsp cayenne
  • 2 tbsp yellow mustard, as a binder
  • 2 cups eastern North Carolina sauce (Chapter 56), for mopping and tossing
  • Hickory chunks ×5
  • 16 soft buns
  1. Score the fat cap and rub with mustard, then the spice mix. Refrigerate overnight, uncovered.
  2. Build the snake with hickory on the first third and a water pan in the middle. Stabilise at 250–275°F.
  3. Shoulder on fat side up. Do not open the lid for four hours.
  4. From hour four, mop with the eastern sauce every hour.
  5. The stall comes at 160°F and can last three hours. Wait it out for better bark, or wrap in butcher paper to push through.
  6. 203°F, and the blade bone must pull out clean with no resistance. That bone test matters more than the number.
  7. Rest wrapped, in towels, in a cooler for 1 to 2 hours.
  8. Pull by hand with gloves, discarding any large lumps of unrendered fat but keeping every piece of bark and chopping it through the meat.
  9. Toss immediately with a cup of eastern sauce while the pork is hot enough to absorb it. This is the seasoning step, not a garnish.
To the table · communalA big tray of pork, a basket of buns, both slaws in bowls and the four sauces lined up in bottles with their names written on masking tape — the flight is the point of the menu, so make it legible. People build their own and try two. Put slaw on the sandwich, which is the Carolina way, and say so.
Red Slaw and White Slaw
Sides · Serves 12 · 20 min

Red slaw has no mayonnaise in it at all — it is cabbage dressed with Lexington dip, and in the Piedmont it goes on the sandwich rather than beside it. Make both and let people compare.

  • Red slaw
  • 1 green cabbage, chopped fine, not shredded
  • 1 cup Lexington dip (Chapter 56) · 2 tbsp sugar · 1 tsp salt · black pepper
  • White slaw
  • 1 green cabbage and 4 carrots, shredded
  • ¾ cup mayonnaise · 3 tbsp cider vinegar · 2 tbsp sugar
  • 1 tsp celery seed · salt and pepper
  1. Chop the red slaw cabbage finely with a knife rather than shredding — the texture is coarser and it holds up to the vinegar.
  2. Dress both and rest at least 2 hours. Red slaw improves overnight; white slaw does not.
Collard Greens
Side · Serves 12 · 3 hr
  • 3 lb collard greens, stems stripped, leaves torn
  • 1 smoked ham hock · ½ lb bacon, chopped
  • 2 onions, diced · 6 cloves garlic
  • 8 cups stock or water · ¼ cup cider vinegar
  • 1 tbsp sugar · red pepper flakes · salt
  1. Render the bacon in a Dutch oven, soften the onions and garlic in the fat, then add the hock and stock.
  2. Simmer 1 hour to draw flavour from the hock before the greens go in.
  3. Add the collards in batches — they collapse to a fraction of their volume.
  4. Move to the indirect side at 325°F and cook 2 to 3 hours, until completely tender. Undercooked collards are tough and bitter; there is no such thing as overcooking them.
  5. Pull the meat off the hock and return it. Finish with vinegar, sugar, flakes and salt.
  6. Serve the greens with their liquid — the pot likker — and cornbread or a bun for soaking it.
Hush Puppies
Side · Makes 30 · 25 min
  • 2 cups fine cornmeal · 1 cup flour
  • 2 tbsp sugar · 1 tbsp baking powder · 1½ tsp salt
  • 1 onion, grated on the fine side of a box grater
  • 1¼ cups buttermilk · 2 eggs
  • Frying oil, 3 inches deep
  1. Whisk the dry ingredients, then stir in the onion, buttermilk and eggs to a thick, sticky batter. Rest 15 minutes so the cornmeal hydrates.
  2. Heat the oil in a Dutch oven to 350°F and hold it there.
  3. Drop rounded tablespoons in, six or eight at a time. They will turn themselves over when the underside sets — if they do not, nudge them.
  4. 3 to 4 minutes, until deep gold. Drain on a rack, never on paper, and salt immediately.
  5. Fry them last. They are perfect for ten minutes and ordinary after thirty.
Banana Pudding
Dessert · Serves 12 · Make ahead
  • 1 can (14 oz) sweetened condensed milk · 1½ cups cold water
  • 1 packet instant vanilla pudding, or a scratch custard of 4 yolks, ⅓ cup sugar, 3 tbsp cornstarch and 2½ cups milk
  • 2 cups heavy cream, whipped
  • 6 ripe bananas, sliced
  • 1 box vanilla wafers
  1. Whisk the condensed milk and water into the pudding base and chill until set, or cook the scratch custard and cool it completely.
  2. Fold in the whipped cream.
  3. Layer in a deep dish: wafers, bananas, cream, and repeat, finishing with cream and a border of wafers standing on end.
  4. Refrigerate at least 6 hours, ideally overnight. The wafers must soften into cake — a banana pudding eaten the same day is a different and worse dessert.
To the table · in the dishServed from the dish it set in, with a big spoon, into bowls. Do not attempt neat portions; it will not hold a shape and trying makes it look worse. Crushed wafers over the top just before serving so some crunch survives.
Sweet Tea, Cheerwine & Bourbon
Drinks · Serves 12
  • Sweet tea (Chapter 26), doubled
  • Cheerwine or another cherry soda, very cold
  • Bourbon · lemons · mint
  1. Cheerwine is the North Carolina soda and the correct thing to hand someone at a pig picking. Serve it in the bottle.
  2. Sweet tea in a glass jug with a separate ice bucket. Bourbon on the table for people to add to either.
  3. Also pour: cold lager. A vinegar-forward menu wants nothing complicated.

Philadelphia Roast Pork

Autumn · Casual · Two kettles · Rotisserie + pizza stone

Philadelphia's actual sandwich, as opposed to the one tourists queue for: rotisserie pork shoulder sliced thin, sharp provolone, garlicky broccoli rabe, and long hots, on a seeded roll you bake yourself. Soft pretzels to start and a lemon water ice to finish.

The menu

  • Soft pretzels · Roasted long hots with provolone
  • Roast pork Italian on seeded rolls
  • Broccoli rabe · Sharp provolone · pan jus for dipping
  • Lemon water ice

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: lump with 2 oak chunks. This is a roast, so keep the smoke light.

Kettle A — the rotisserie. Split baskets at 325°F, drip pan beneath catching the jus, which is the most important by-product of the menu. A 6 lb boneless shoulder takes 3 to 3½ hours to 190°F — past medium, because it gets sliced thin and wants to be tender rather than pink.

Kettle B — stone, then direct. Stone at 450°F for the pretzels and the rolls. Then a direct corner for the long hots and the broccoli rabe.

Charcoal: about 3 chimneys on A, 2.5 on B.

Soft Pretzels
Appetizer · Makes 12 · 2 hr

The alkaline bath is what makes a pretzel a pretzel — it raises the surface pH so the crust browns deep mahogany and develops that specific flavour. Lye gives the authentic result; a baking-soda bath gets you most of the way with none of the hazard.

  • 4 cups bread flour · 1½ tsp salt · 1 tbsp brown sugar
  • 2¼ tsp instant yeast · 1⅓ cups warm water · 3 tbsp melted butter
  • The bath
  • Either: ⅔ cup baking soda boiled in 8 cups water
  • Or: 8 cups cold water with 2 tbsp food-grade lye (gloves and goggles, always add lye to water)
  • To finish
  • 1 egg, beaten · coarse pretzel salt · brown mustard
  1. Mix and knead 8 minutes to a firm, smooth dough — stiffer than a bread dough. Rise 1 hour.
  2. Divide into 12, roll each into a 24-inch rope, thicker in the middle, and twist into a pretzel.
  3. Dip each into the bath for 20 seconds, turning once, and lift out onto parchment.
  4. Egg wash, slash the fat belly of each pretzel once, and salt heavily.
  5. Bake on the stone at 450°F for 12 to 15 minutes, until deeply browned — pale pretzels taste of nothing.
Lye

Food-grade lye is genuinely caustic. Wear gloves and eye protection, use a stainless or plastic bowl and never aluminium, always add lye to water and never the reverse, and keep it away from children and pets. It becomes completely inert during baking. If any of that gives you pause, use the baking soda bath — the result is 85 percent as good.

To the table · basketWarm, in a basket, with brown mustard in a bowl for dipping. In Philadelphia they come stacked and slightly stuck together in a paper bag, which is a better serving vessel than anything you own.
Seeded Hoagie Rolls
Side · Makes 8 · 3 hr

A proper Philadelphia roll is crisp outside and soft inside and — critically — strong enough to hold wet pork and greens without dissolving. Steam in the first minutes gives the crust.

  • 5 cups bread flour · 2 tsp salt · 1 tbsp sugar
  • 2¼ tsp instant yeast · 1¾ cups warm water · 2 tbsp olive oil
  • Sesame seeds · 1 egg white, beaten with 1 tbsp water
  1. Mix and knead 10 minutes. Rise 1½ hours, or overnight in the fridge for better flavour.
  2. Divide into 8, shape into 7-inch torpedoes with tapered ends, and proof 45 minutes on parchment.
  3. Brush with egg white and roll the tops in sesame.
  4. Slash each roll once lengthwise. Bake on the stone at 450°F, and throw a small cup of water onto the charcoal grate as you close the lid — the burst of steam is what gives a crisp, blistered crust.
  5. 18 to 22 minutes, until deep gold and hollow-sounding.
  6. Cool completely before splitting, or the crumb tears.
Rotisserie Roast Pork Italian
Main · Serves 8 · 3½ hr
  • 1 boneless pork shoulder, 6 lb, tied into an even cylinder
  • 8 cloves garlic, minced · 2 tbsp fennel seed, crushed
  • 2 tbsp chopped rosemary · 1 tbsp red pepper flakes
  • 2 tbsp coarse salt · 1 tbsp black pepper · 3 tbsp olive oil
  • 2 cups chicken stock, in the drip pan
  • 1½ lb sharp provolone, sliced thin
  1. Stab the shoulder all over and work the garlic, fennel, rosemary, flakes, salt, pepper and oil into every cut. Refrigerate overnight, uncovered.
  2. Tie into an even cylinder — an irregular shoulder will not turn cleanly.
  3. Spit, balance, and roast indirect at 325°F over a drip pan with the stock in it.
  4. 3 to 3½ hours to 190°F. This goes well past pink deliberately; it is sliced thin, and at 145°F it would be chewy rather than tender.
  5. Rest 30 minutes. Meanwhile strain the drip pan, skim the fat, and keep the jus hot — that liquid is what the sandwich is dipped in.
  6. Slice as thinly as you possibly can, across the grain, and drop the slices into the warm jus to sit until served.
To the table · handBuilt to order and handed over wrapped in paper, not plated. Pork lifted dripping from the jus into a split roll, provolone laid on the hot meat so it melts, broccoli rabe piled on, long hots on top. A cup of jus for dipping on the side. Napkins, plural, per person.
Broccoli Rabe & Roasted Long Hots
Sides · Serves 8 · 20 min
  • 3 bunches broccoli rabe, tough stem ends trimmed
  • 6 cloves garlic, sliced · ⅓ cup olive oil · 1 tsp red pepper flakes
  • Lemon · salt
  • Long hots
  • 12 Italian long hot peppers · 2 tbsp olive oil · salt
  1. Blanch the rabe 2 minutes in heavily salted boiling water and refresh in cold. This is what removes the worst of its bitterness, and skipping it is why home versions are harsh.
  2. Squeeze dry, then sauté hard in a cast-iron pan with the garlic, oil and flakes until the edges catch, 5 minutes. Lemon and salt at the end.
  3. Grill the long hots whole over direct heat, turning, until blistered and collapsing — 8 to 10 minutes. Salt while hot.
  4. Long hots vary wildly in heat from one pepper to the next. That unpredictability is considered part of the experience in Philadelphia, and it is worth warning people.
Lemon Water Ice
Dessert · Serves 8 · Make ahead

Philadelphia water ice is not a sorbet and not a granita — it is smoother than one and finer than the other, which comes from stirring it repeatedly as it freezes rather than churning it once.

  • 1½ cups sugar · 1½ cups water
  • 1½ cups lemon juice, about 10 lemons
  • Zest of 3 lemons · pinch of salt
  1. Simmer the sugar, water and zest into a syrup, 5 minutes. Cool completely, then strain and add the lemon juice and salt.
  2. Freeze in a shallow metal tray. Stir hard with a fork every 30 minutes for the first 3 hours, scraping the frozen edges into the middle.
  3. After that it can sit. The repeated stirring is what makes it fine-textured rather than icy.
  4. Let it soften 10 minutes before scooping.
To the table · bowlPaper cups and flat wooden spoons if you want to be accurate about it, small bowls if you do not. Scooped into rough scrapes rather than neat spheres — water ice is not ice cream and should not look like it.
The Citywide & Birch Beer
Drinks · Serves 8
  • A cheap cold lager and a shot of whiskey, per person — the citywide special
  • Birch beer, very cold (the Pennsylvania soda; root beer is a poor substitute)
  • Lemon and seltzer
  1. The citywide is a can and a shot, handed over together, and there is no ceremony to it whatsoever.
  2. Birch beer in bottles on ice — it is sharper and more medicinal than root beer and it cuts fatty pork far better.
  3. Also pour: a rough Italian red, which is what the roast pork shops actually have on the shelf.
