Down East Clambake

High summer · Coastal New England · Two kettles · Deflector · Homemade buns

The kettle becomes a steamer: seaweed on the bottom, shellfish on top, lid down. Meanwhile the second grill bakes the split-top rolls the lobster is going into. It is the most theatrical menu in this book — you lift the foil and a wall of briny steam comes off it — and the one most worth doing on newspaper spread over a table outdoors.

The menu

  • Grilled littlenecks · New England clam dip
  • The bake — lobster, steamers, mussels, linguiça, potatoes, corn
  • Lobster rolls, both ways, on split-top buns (Chapter 19)
  • Cider-vinegar slaw · Drawn butter · Bread-and-butter pickles
  • Blueberry grunt (batter, Chapter 21)

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: hardwood lump with 2 oak chunks. The star ingredient is rockweed — ask your fishmonger and they will usually throw it in with a lobster order. No rockweed? Use 3 cups water, a bottle of beer and a handful of lemon halves.

Kettle A — the steamer. A full chimney of lump spread wide and hot, with a large disposable roasting pan or deep stockpot set on the grate. All vents open, kettle lid closed over the top. 25 to 35 minutes.

Kettle B — buns, then dessert. Deflector at 350°F for the split-top rolls, about 20 minutes. The grunt goes into a Dutch oven here afterwards, and a corner of direct heat toasts the buttered bun faces at the end.

Charcoal: 2 chimneys on A — you need real steam — and 1.5 on B.

Grilled Littlenecks
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 8 min
  • 3 dozen littleneck clams, scrubbed
  • 1 stick butter, melted with 3 cloves minced garlic
  • Chopped parsley · lemon · black pepper
  1. Purge the clams in cold salted water for 20 minutes so they spit out sand. Discard any that stay open when tapped.
  2. Lay them straight on a hot grate, hinge down. Lid off.
  3. 4 to 8 minutes, until they pop open — grab them with tongs as they go, and try not to spill the liquor.
  4. Spoon garlic butter into each, scatter parsley, squeeze lemon. Anything still shut after the others have opened goes in the bin.
New England Clam Dip
Appetizer · Makes 3 cups · No cook
  • 8 oz cream cheese, softened · 1 cup sour cream
  • 2 cans (6.5 oz each) chopped clams, drained, 2 tbsp juice reserved
  • 3 scallions, minced · 1 tsp Worcestershire
  • 1 tsp lemon juice · ½ tsp Old Bay · hot sauce · black pepper
  1. Beat the cream cheese smooth, then fold in everything else with the reserved clam juice.
  2. Refrigerate at least 4 hours — it is flat straight away and excellent the next day.
  3. Serve with grilled bread or kettle chips.
The Bake
Main · Serves 8 · 35 min

The layering principle is the recipe: densest and slowest nearest the heat, shellfish on top so it doesn't overcook while the potatoes catch up. Do not stir, do not peek before 25 minutes.

  • 6 live lobsters, 1¼ lb each
  • 4 lb steamer clams, purged · 2 lb mussels, debearded
  • 1½ lb linguiça, in 3-inch lengths
  • 4 lb red bliss potatoes, halved if large
  • 3 onions, quartered · 8 ears corn in the husk, silk removed
  • A large armful of rockweed, rinsed
  • 3 cups water · 2 lemons, halved · 2 tbsp Old Bay
  1. Set a large disposable roasting pan or stockpot on the grate over a full, hot fire. Pour in the water, lemons and Old Bay.
  2. Layer in order: a bed of rockweed, then potatoes and onions, more weed, then linguiça and corn, more weed, then clams and mussels, and finally the lobsters on top.
  3. Cover tightly with heavy foil or the pot lid, then close the kettle lid over the whole thing with every vent wide open.
  4. 25 to 35 minutes. It's done when the lobsters are uniformly bright red, the clams have opened, and a knife goes through a potato with no resistance.
  5. Carry the pan to a newspaper-covered table and tip it out. Serve with drawn butter and a lot of napkins.
Shellfish

Discard any clam or mussel that was already open before cooking and doesn't close when tapped, and any that stays shut after the rest have opened. This is not a superstition.

Lobster Rolls, Both Ways
Main · Makes 10 · 20 min

The Maine and Connecticut argument will never be settled. Make both — split the picked meat down the middle and let people take a side.

  • Meat from 6 lobsters, about 3 cups, in large chunks
  • 10 split-top rolls (Chapter 19)
  • 1 stick butter, for griddling the buns
  • Connecticut — warm
  • 6 tbsp butter, melted · lemon · a pinch of salt
  • Maine — cold
  • ⅓ cup mayonnaise · 1 stalk celery, finely diced
  • 2 tsp lemon juice · 1 tbsp chives · salt and pepper
  1. Pick the lobster while it's still warm. Keep the pieces big — knuckle and claw whole, tail in two or three chunks. Shredded lobster is a waste of lobster.
  2. For Connecticut: warm half the meat gently in melted butter in a pan at the edge of the fire, no more than 2 minutes, then lemon and salt.
  3. For Maine: fold the other half with mayonnaise, celery, lemon and chives. Dress lightly — you should see lobster, not mayonnaise.
  4. Butter the flat sides of the split-top rolls generously and griddle them in cast iron over direct heat, 90 seconds a side, until deep gold.
  5. Fill through the split top and serve immediately.
Cider-Vinegar Slaw & Drawn Butter
Sides · Serves 8
  • 1 green cabbage and 3 carrots, shredded
  • ½ cup cider vinegar · 3 tbsp sugar · 2 tbsp mayonnaise
  • 1 tsp celery seed · 1 tsp salt · black pepper
  • Drawn butter
  • 3 sticks butter · 2 lemons
  1. Toss the slaw at least 2 hours ahead. The sharp, barely creamy dressing is the right foil for a table of butter.
  2. Melt the butter slowly in a small pot at the edge of the fire. Let it sit off the heat for a few minutes, then spoon the clear gold middle layer into ramekins, leaving the milky solids behind.
  3. Lemon wedges on every plate.
Blueberry Grunt
Dessert · Serves 8 · 30 min

A steamed New England dessert — fruit bubbling below, soft dumplings dropped on top and cooked under a closed lid until they puff. The name is said to come from the noise the fruit makes.

  • 2 lb wild blueberries
  • ⅔ cup sugar · 2 tbsp cornstarch
  • Juice and zest of 1 lemon · ½ tsp cinnamon · 2 tbsp water
  • 1 batch grunt dumpling batter (Chapter 21)
  • Heavy cream, to pour
  1. Combine the berries, sugar, cornstarch, lemon, cinnamon and water in a Dutch oven and bring to a simmer over indirect heat, about 10 minutes.
  2. Drop the batter over the surface in eight spoonfuls, leaving gaps for steam to escape.
  3. Cover tightly and cook, lid on and not lifted, for 20 to 25 minutes, until a skewer in a dumpling comes out clean.
  4. Serve warm in bowls with cold cream poured over. The dumplings should be pale and soft — this is not a cobbler and should not be browned.
Switchel & Dark 'n' Stormy
Drinks · Serves 8

Switchel is the original New England field drink — vinegar, ginger and maple, drunk by haymakers in July. It sounds wrong and it is remarkably good in heat.

  • Switchel
  • 8 cups water · ½ cup cider vinegar
  • ⅓ cup maple syrup · 3-inch ginger, grated · pinch of salt
  • Dark 'n' Stormy · per glass
  • 2 oz dark rum · 4 oz ginger beer · lime
  • Blueberry lemonade
  • 1 cup blueberries, crushed · 1½ cups lemon juice · ¾ cup sugar · 6 cups water
  1. Stir the switchel together and refrigerate at least 4 hours; strain if you prefer it without the ginger.
  2. Build the Dark 'n' Stormy over ice with the rum floated on top of the ginger beer, and squeeze a lime over.
  3. Also pour: Narragansett or any cold New England lager; unoaked Chardonnay or Muscadet with the lobster.

Santa Maria Style

Late spring · California Central Coast · Two kettles · Deflector

California's own barbecue tradition, and nothing like the smoked stuff. Tri-tip seasoned with salt, pepper and garlic, cooked over red oak coals, sliced thin, and served with pinquito beans, grilled garlic bread and a salsa that contains celery and Worcestershire. It's a ranch-hand meal that happens to be elegant.

The menu

  • Grilled artichokes with lemon aioli · a Central Coast board
  • Santa Maria tri-tip (rub, Chapter 23)
  • Pinquito beans · Santa Maria salsa (Chapter 22) · Grilled sweet onion and asparagus · Garlic bread
  • Strawberry shortcake on grilled biscuits (Chapter 21)

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: lump charcoal plus red oak chunks or splits. Red oak is the entire point of this style — mild, sweet and clean, and what makes tri-tip taste like Santa Maria rather than like generic grilled beef. White oak is the substitute.

Kettle A — the beef. A full chimney banked to one side with 3 red oak chunks on top. Sear each roast fat-cap-down 4 to 5 minutes a side over direct, then move to indirect with the lid on, vents open, 25 to 35 minutes to 130–135°F.

Kettle B — beans, biscuits, vegetables. Deflector at 325°F; the beans go on three hours ahead. Biscuits at 400°F for 15 to 18 minutes. Artichokes, onions and asparagus over a direct corner. Garlic bread last, cut-side down, 90 seconds.

Charcoal: 1.5 chimneys on A, 2.5 on B — the beans run long.

Grilled Artichokes with Lemon Aioli
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 45 min
  • 4 large artichokes, halved, chokes scooped out
  • 4 lemons · olive oil · salt
  • Aioli
  • 1 cup mayonnaise · 3 cloves garlic, grated to a paste
  • Juice and zest of 1 lemon · 1 tbsp olive oil · black pepper
  1. Rub the cut faces with lemon immediately — artichoke browns within seconds.
  2. Seal the halves in heavy foil packets with a lemon half, a slug of oil and a splash of water. Set on the indirect side and steam 30 to 35 minutes, until a knife goes into the heart easily.
  3. Open the packets, brush the cut faces with oil, and grill cut-side down over direct heat 4 to 5 minutes until charred.
  4. Whisk the aioli and serve alongside for dipping the leaves.
Santa Maria Tri-Tip
Main · Serves 8 · 45 min

The one thing that separates good tri-tip from chewy tri-tip is the slicing. A tri-tip has two grain directions meeting at a bend, and cutting straight through the whole roast means half of it is cut the wrong way.

  • 2 tri-tip roasts, 2½–3 lb each, fat cap left on
  • Santa Maria rub (Chapter 23) — 1 tbsp per pound
  • Red oak chunks
  • Santa Maria salsa (Chapter 22), to serve
  1. Rub both roasts and refrigerate uncovered 8 to 24 hours. Bring to room temperature before cooking.
  2. Bank a full chimney to one side, red oak on top, and let the wood catch and settle.
  3. Sear fat-cap-down first, 4 to 5 minutes, then the other side 4 minutes. Expect flare-ups; move the meat, don't spray water.
  4. Move to the indirect side, lid on, top vent above the meat. 25 to 35 minutes to 130–135°F.
  5. Rest 15 minutes.
  6. Cut the roast in half at the bend where the grain changes direction. Then slice each half thinly, across its own grain, at a slight angle.
Pinquito Beans
Side · Serves 8 · 3 hr

Pinquitos are a small pink bean grown almost exclusively in the Santa Maria Valley. They hold their shape where other beans collapse, which is why the dish is what it is. Small pink beans are the nearest substitute; pintos are not.

  • 2 lb pinquito beans, soaked overnight
  • ½ lb bacon, chopped · 1 onion, diced · 6 cloves garlic
  • 1 cup tomato purée · 2 tbsp brown sugar
  • 1 tbsp dry mustard · 1 tsp chilli powder · 1 tsp cumin
  • 6 cups water or stock · salt
  1. Render the bacon in a Dutch oven over direct heat, then soften the onion and garlic in the fat.
  2. Add the drained beans and water and bring to a simmer.
  3. Move to the indirect side over the deflector at 325°F, lid on, and cook 2 to 2½ hours, topping up water as needed.
  4. Stir in the tomato, sugar, mustard and spices in the last 45 minutes.
  5. Salt only at the end. Salted early, the skins toughen and the beans take twice as long.
Garlic Bread & Grilled Vegetables
Sides · Serves 8
  • 2 loaves sweet French bread, split lengthwise
  • 1½ sticks butter, softened · 6 cloves garlic, grated · parsley · salt
  • 2 sweet onions, in thick rounds · 2 bunches asparagus
  • Olive oil, salt, pepper
  1. Mash the butter with garlic, parsley and salt and spread thickly on the cut faces.
  2. Grill cut-side down over moderate direct heat for 90 seconds to 2 minutes, watching constantly — garlic bread goes from gold to black in about fifteen seconds. Cut into fingers.
  3. Skewer the onion rounds crosswise so they don't fall apart. Grill onions 5 minutes a side, asparagus 3 minutes a side, both oiled and salted.
Strawberry Shortcake on Grilled Biscuits
Dessert · Serves 8 · 25 min
  • 1 batch buttermilk biscuits, made with ¼ cup sugar (Chapter 21)
  • 3 lb strawberries, hulled and sliced
  • ⅓ cup sugar · 1 tbsp lemon juice · a few basil leaves, torn
  • 2 cups heavy cream, whipped to soft peaks with 2 tbsp sugar
  • 2 tbsp melted butter, for the biscuits
  1. Toss the strawberries with sugar, lemon and basil and leave them at least an hour — the syrup they give up is the sauce.
  2. Split the baked biscuits, brush the cut faces with butter, and grill them cut-side down for 60 to 90 seconds over the last of the direct heat.
  3. Build: bottom half, berries and syrup, a heap of cream, top half, more berries.
  4. Whip the cream barely sweet — the berries are doing that job.
Meyer Lemon and Mint Iced Tea
Drink · Serves 8
  • 6 black tea bags · 8 cups water
  • Juice of 4 Meyer lemons · ½ cup sugar · a large handful of mint
  1. Steep the tea 5 minutes only. Stir the sugar in while hot, then add the mint and let it infuse as the tea cools.
  2. Strain, add the lemon juice, and chill hard. Serve over ice with a lemon wheel.
  3. Also pour: Central Coast Pinot Noir from the Santa Rita Hills if you want finesse, Paso Robles Zinfandel if you want to meet the beef head-on; a crisp California pilsner through the afternoon.

New Orleans Winter

Winter · Mardi Gras or a cold Saturday · Two kettles · Deflector

Oysters bubbling in garlic butter over a ferocious fire, a Dutch oven of jambalaya absorbing pecan smoke under a deflector, and bananas Foster flamed at the table. Rich and loud, and built for a night nobody wants to stand outside for long — most of the cooking happens lid-down and unattended.

The menu

  • Char-grilled oysters · Grilled boudin with Creole mustard
  • Chicken and andouille jambalaya · BBQ shrimp
  • Maque choux · Grilled okra · Rémoulade slaw (Chapter 22) · French bread
  • Bananas Foster

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: lump charcoal with pecan chunks — the Louisiana wood, mild and nutty. Hickory if you must.

Kettle A — oysters and shrimp. A full chimney spread edge to edge, as hot as you can make it. Oysters go on the half shell and take 5 to 7 minutes; the cast-iron shrimp pan follows over the same coals.

Kettle B — jambalaya. Brown the andouille and chicken over direct heat first, then slide the deflector in at 325°F and cook the rice covered for 45 to 55 minutes with a pecan chunk on the coals. Maque choux in a second skillet alongside. Bananas Foster here at the end.

Charcoal: 2 chimneys per kettle. Oysters want ferocious heat and burn through fuel fast.

Char-Grilled Oysters
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 7 min per batch

The fire has to be genuinely fierce. The dish works because the butter boils over the shell edge and catches on the coals, which is where the flavour comes from — a timid fire just poaches the oyster.

  • 4 dozen oysters, shucked, on the half shell
  • Compound butter
  • 2 sticks butter, softened
  • 6 cloves garlic, grated · 2 tbsp chopped parsley
  • 1 tbsp Creole seasoning · 1 tsp cayenne · juice of 1 lemon
  • To finish
  • 1 cup grated Parmesan mixed with ½ cup Romano
  • Lemon wedges, hot sauce, French bread
  1. Mash the butter ingredients together. This can be made days ahead and rolled in cling film.
  2. Set the oysters directly on a grate over a full, hot fire. Spoon a generous knob of butter onto each.
  3. When the butter melts and starts to bubble — about 3 minutes — scatter the cheese over the top.
  4. Total 5 to 7 minutes. They're done when the butter is boiling hard, the cheese is browning and the edges of the oyster have ruffled.
  5. Serve immediately with bread for mopping the shells. Work in batches of a dozen and hand them out as they come off.
Chicken and Andouille Jambalaya
Main · Serves 8 · 1¼ hr

This is the Creole version, with tomato. Once the rice goes in, do not stir it — stirring releases starch and turns jambalaya into a risotto, which it isn't.

  • 2 lb andouille, sliced ½ inch thick
  • 2 lb boneless chicken thighs, in large chunks
  • 2 tbsp Creole seasoning, plus more
  • 2 onions, 2 green peppers, 3 stalks celery — the trinity, all diced
  • 6 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 can diced tomatoes
  • 3 cups long-grain rice
  • 4½ cups chicken stock
  • 2 bay leaves · 1 tsp thyme · cayenne to taste
  • Green onions and parsley, to finish
  1. Season the chicken heavily. Brown the andouille in a Dutch oven over direct heat and set aside; brown the chicken in the rendered fat and set aside.
  2. Cook the trinity in the fat until soft and starting to catch, 10 minutes. Add the garlic, then the tomatoes, bay and thyme.
  3. Return the meat, stir in the rice to coat, then add the stock. Taste the liquid now and make it slightly saltier than you want the finished dish — the rice will absorb it.
  4. Bring to a boil, cover, and move to the indirect side over the deflector at 325°F, with a pecan chunk on the coals.
  5. 45 to 55 minutes, undisturbed. No stirring, no lifting the lid.
  6. Rest 10 minutes off the heat, then fork through with green onion and parsley.
BBQ Shrimp
Main · Serves 8 · 12 min

Nothing to do with barbecue sauce. This is a New Orleans skillet dish of shrimp in an absurd quantity of butter, black pepper and Worcestershire, and the bread you mop it with is as important as the shrimp.

  • 2 lb head-on shrimp, or large shell-on
  • 2 sticks butter, cubed
  • ⅓ cup Worcestershire
  • 2 tbsp coarsely ground black pepper
  • 6 cloves garlic, minced · 1 tbsp Creole seasoning
  • 1 tsp cayenne · juice of 2 lemons · 1 bay leaf
  • Crusty French bread
  1. The pepper quantity is not a misprint. This is a pepper dish.
  2. Melt a third of the butter in a cast-iron skillet over direct heat. Add the garlic, pepper, Creole seasoning, cayenne and bay and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the Worcestershire and lemon and let it bubble and reduce slightly.
  4. Add the shrimp in one layer. Cook 2 to 3 minutes a side, until just opaque.
  5. Pull the pan to the cool side and swirl in the remaining cold butter a few cubes at a time so the sauce emulsifies rather than splitting.
  6. Serve in the pan, shells on, with bread and a stack of napkins. Peeling them yourself is the tradition.
Maque Choux & Grilled Okra
Sides · Serves 8
  • 6 ears corn, kernels cut off, cobs scraped for the milk
  • 1 onion and 1 green pepper, diced · 3 cloves garlic
  • 4 tbsp butter · ½ cup heavy cream
  • 1 tsp Creole seasoning · cayenne · green onion
  • Okra
  • 1 lb okra, whole · oil, Creole seasoning, lemon
  1. Cook the onion and pepper in butter in a skillet over direct heat until soft, then the garlic.
  2. Add the corn kernels and the milky scrapings from the cobs and cook 10 minutes, letting some of it catch and brown.
  3. Add the cream and simmer until thickened. Finish with green onion.
  4. Grill the okra whole over direct heat, 3 to 4 minutes a side — left whole and cooked hot, it chars rather than going slimy.
Bananas Foster
Dessert · Serves 8 · 10 min
  • 8 bananas, halved lengthwise then crosswise
  • 1 stick butter · 1 cup dark brown sugar
  • 1 tsp cinnamon · pinch of salt
  • ¼ cup banana liqueur (optional)
  • ⅓ cup dark rum
  • Vanilla ice cream
  1. Melt the butter and brown sugar in a cast-iron skillet over moderate direct heat until it bubbles into a sauce, 3 minutes. Stir in cinnamon and salt.
  2. Lay the bananas in cut-side down and cook 1 to 2 minutes, spooning sauce over. They should soften, not disintegrate.
  3. Pull the pan off the fire. Pour the rum around the edge, then tip the pan slightly toward the coals or use a long lighter to ignite it.
  4. Let the flame burn out on its own — about 30 seconds — swirling gently.
  5. Spoon over ice cream immediately, while the sauce is still hot enough to melt it at the edges.
Flaming safely

Pan off the heat before the rum goes in. Never pour spirits from the bottle into a lit pan — the flame can travel up the stream. Measure into a cup first, keep faces and hair back, and have the lid to hand.

Pacific Northwest

Early autumn · One kettle · Pizza stone + cedar planks

The most restrained menu in this book. Cedar-planked salmon, crab cracked over newspaper, foraged mushrooms and blackberries. Alder is the regional smoke — gentler than anything in the hickory family and what the coastal tribes have smoked salmon over for centuries.

The menu

  • Grilled Dungeness crab · Chanterelle and hazelnut toasts
  • Cedar-plank salmon (brine, Chapter 24)
  • Seared wild mushrooms · Grilled delicata squash · Apple-fennel slaw
  • Marionberry hand pies (pastry, Chapter 21)

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: lump charcoal with alder chunks. Cedar planks are a cooking surface, not fuel — soak them two hours minimum and keep a spray bottle handy.

One kettle. Two-zone with an alder chunk on the coals, running about 375°F. Crab and mushrooms first over the direct side. Then the planked salmon on the indirect side, lid down, 20 to 30 minutes to 125–130°F. Squash alongside. Once the salmon is off, the fire has fallen to exactly hand-pie temperature: slide the pizza stone in, give it 15 minutes to heat, and bake the pies at 400°F.

Charcoal: about 2 chimneys total.

Grilled Dungeness Crab
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 10 min
  • 3 cooked Dungeness crabs, cleaned and split into halves or quarters
  • 1 stick butter, browned
  • 2 tbsp chopped tarragon · 2 cloves garlic · lemon · flaky salt
  1. Brown the butter in a small pan on the grill until the solids smell nutty and turn golden. Off the heat, add garlic, tarragon and lemon.
  2. Crack the shells lightly with the back of a knife so the butter gets in and eating is easier.
  3. Brush the cut faces generously and grill cut-side down, 4 to 5 minutes. The crab is already cooked — this is for flavour and warmth only.
  4. Serve on newspaper with crackers, picks, more butter and lemon.

Cedar-Plank Salmon
Main · Serves 8 · 30 min plus brining
  • 1 whole side of salmon, about 4 lb, pin bones removed
  • Salmon brine (Chapter 24)
  • 2–3 untreated cedar planks, soaked 2 hours
  • Glaze
  • ⅓ cup maple syrup · 2 tbsp Dijon · 1 tbsp cider vinegar · black pepper
  • Lemon, dill
  1. Brine 45 minutes only, then rinse and rest the fish on a rack in the fridge 1 to 2 hours, until the surface is tacky rather than wet. That tacky film — the pellicle — is what takes on smoke and holds the glaze.
  2. Whisk the glaze and brush half of it on.
  3. Set the salmon skin-side down on the soaked planks and place on the indirect side with an alder chunk on the coals. Lid down, about 375°F.
  4. 20 to 30 minutes. Brush on the rest of the glaze at the halfway mark.
  5. Pull at 125–130°F at the thickest point. It carries up as it rests, and there is no recovering from overcooked salmon.
  6. Serve on the plank with lemon and dill.
Planks catch fire

Edges will char and sometimes flame. That's fine — spray them and carry on. If a whole plank goes up, slide a second soaked plank underneath and move the fish across.

Wisconsin Tailgate

Late autumn · Football Saturday · Two kettles · Deflector · Homemade buns

A brat fry done properly: links simmered in a beer-and-onion bath, charred on the grate, then returned to the bath to hold — which means you feed eight people at their own pace instead of all at once. Homemade buns, cheese in three forms, and a brandy old fashioned.

The menu

  • Grilled cheese curds · Summer sausage and cheddar on rye
  • Beer brats on homemade brat buns (Chapter 19) · Butter burgers
  • German potato salad · Sauerkraut and grilled onions · a mustard bar
  • Frozen custard with warm Door County cherry sauce

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: briquettes, steadier in the cold, plus apple or maple chunks. Add 25 percent if it's below 40°F and shelter the kettle from wind.

Kettle A — brats and burgers. Two-zone with a disposable foil pan of beer, butter and sliced onion simmering on the indirect side. A cast-iron plancha on the direct side handles butter burgers to order.

Kettle B — buns, potato salad, curds. Deflector at 350°F. Buns first, 18 to 22 minutes. Then the potato salad in cast iron. Curds at the very end so they arrive molten. Cherry sauce after dinner.

Charcoal: 2.5 chimneys on A — the bath needs sustained heat — and 2 on B.

Grilled Cheese Curds
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 8 min

Curds must be fresh enough to squeak against your teeth. Older curds have lost the moisture that makes them worth eating and will simply weep grease.

  • 1½ lb fresh cheese curds, large ones
  • 1 tbsp neutral oil
  • Beer mustard dip
  • ½ cup whole-grain mustard · ¼ cup beer
  • 2 tbsp honey · 1 tsp cider vinegar
  1. Pat the curds dry and toss them lightly in oil.
  2. Spread them in a single layer in a preheated cast-iron pan over medium direct heat — high heat splits them.
  3. 3 to 4 minutes, until the undersides are browned and blistered and the insides have just gone soft. Flip once with a spatula.
  4. Serve immediately in the pan with the mustard dip. They are excellent hot and unpleasant cold, so make them last.
Beer Brats
Main · Serves 8 · 40 min

The order matters and Wisconsin argues about it. This is the version that works best for a crowd: simmer first, char second, then back into the bath to hold — which means they stay hot and juicy for an hour while people drift in.

  • 16 fresh bratwurst
  • 4 bottles lager
  • 1 stick butter
  • 4 onions, sliced
  • 1 tsp caraway seed · black pepper
  • 12 brat buns (Chapter 19) · mustards · sauerkraut
  1. Put the beer, butter, onions, caraway and pepper in a disposable foil pan on the indirect side and bring it to a bare simmer.
  2. Add the brats and poach 20 minutes. Never boil — hard boiling bursts the casings and squeezes out the fat.
  3. Move them to the direct side and grill 8 to 10 minutes, turning often, until browned all over and 160°F inside.
  4. Return them to the bath. They will hold for an hour and get better doing it, as will the onions.
  5. Serve in split buns with a heap of the bath onions, sauerkraut and a choice of mustards. Three mustards minimum.
Butter Burgers
Main · Makes 8 · 10 min
  • 2 lb ground beef, 80/20, in 8 loose 4 oz balls
  • 8 tbsp butter, softened, plus more for the buns
  • Salt and pepper · 8 slices American or cheddar
  • Buns, sliced onion, pickle
  1. Don't work the meat. Loose balls, handled as little as possible.
  2. Get the cast-iron plancha very hot. Lay a ball on and smash it flat with a stiff spatula for 10 seconds, then leave it.
  3. Season the top. 2 minutes, until the edges are lacy and brown, then scrape under with the spatula — taking all the crust — and flip.
  4. Cheese on, 60 seconds more.
  5. Butter the toasted bun and lay a full tablespoon of soft butter on top of the patty before you close it. That is the whole idea of a butter burger and halving it defeats the point.
German Potato Salad
Side · Serves 8 · 25 min
  • 4 lb Yukon gold potatoes, boiled and sliced ½ inch thick
  • 1 lb thick bacon, chopped
  • 2 onions, diced
  • ½ cup cider vinegar · ¾ cup stock or the potato water
  • 2 tbsp sugar · 2 tbsp Dijon
  • Parsley and chives · salt and plenty of pepper
  1. Render the bacon crisp in a large cast-iron pan on the indirect side. Lift it out, leaving the fat.
  2. Soften the onions in the fat, then add the vinegar, stock, sugar and mustard and let it bubble into a dressing.
  3. Fold in the warm sliced potatoes and the bacon. The potatoes must be warm — cold ones won't absorb the dressing.
  4. Hold on the indirect side, herbs stirred through at the last moment. Served warm, never chilled.
Frozen Custard with Door County Cherry Sauce
Dessert · Serves 8 · 15 min

The custard is bought, and should be. Frozen custard is a churned, egg-rich institution best made by people with the right machine; your job is the sauce.

  • 2 quarts vanilla frozen custard
  • 1½ lb tart cherries, pitted (frozen is fine)
  • ⅔ cup sugar · 1 tbsp cornstarch
  • Juice of ½ lemon · ¼ tsp almond extract
  • 3 tbsp brandy
  1. Combine the cherries, sugar, cornstarch and lemon in a cast-iron pan over moderate direct heat.
  2. Cook 10 to 12 minutes, stirring, until the fruit has broken down slightly and the sauce is glossy and thick enough to coat a spoon.
  3. Off the heat, stir in the brandy and almond extract.
  4. Spoon warm over scoops of very cold custard.
Brandy Old Fashioned Sweet
Drink · Per glass

Wisconsin's actual state drink, and a source of genuine bafflement everywhere else: brandy instead of whiskey, muddled fruit, and lemon-lime soda on top. Order an "old fashioned" anywhere in the state and this is what arrives.

  • 2 oz brandy (Korbel, traditionally)
  • 1 orange slice · 2 maraschino cherries
  • 1 sugar cube · 3 dashes Angostura · a splash of water
  • Lemon-lime soda, to top · ice
  1. Muddle the orange slice, one cherry, the sugar cube and bitters with a splash of water in the bottom of the glass.
  2. Fill with ice, add the brandy, and top with lemon-lime soda. Garnish with the second cherry.
  3. "Sweet" takes soda; "press" takes half soda and half sparkling water; "sour" takes sour mix. Ask people which they want and they will be impressed.
  4. Also pour: New Glarus Spotted Cow if you can get it across state lines, any good amber ale if you can't. Cold fresh cider and a cherry phosphate for anyone not drinking.

Island Style Luau

Late summer · Graduation or birthday · Two kettles · Rotisserie + deflector · Homemade rolls

Kalua pork smoking under a deflector all day while huli huli chicken turns on the spit, basted only at the very end so the sugar doesn't burn. The pork ends up as sliders on homemade Hawaiian sweet rolls. It is the longest day in this book and almost all of it is unattended.

The menu

  • Ahi poke · Grilled Spam musubi
  • Kalua pork on Hawaiian sweet rolls (Chapter 19) · Huli huli chicken (sauce, Chapter 24)
  • Lomi lomi salmon · Mac salad · Grilled Maui onion and sweet potato · rice
  • Butter mochi and haupia

Grill plan & fuel

Fuel: lump charcoal with kiawe if you can order it — Hawaii's mesquite relative, sweeter and less aggressive. Mesquite is the substitute; use it sparingly. Banana leaves come frozen from any Asian or Latin market.

Kettle A — kalua pork. Deflector in at 275°F, drip pan of water beneath, kiawe in the first two hours. 7 to 8 hours to 200–205°F.

Kettle B — rotisserie, then baking. Split baskets at 350°F; two 4 lb chickens for 75 to 90 minutes to 165°F, glazed only in the final 20 minutes. Once they're off, deflector in at 350°F for the sweet rolls, then the butter mochi.

Charcoal: 4 chimneys on A across the day, 2.5 on B.

Ahi Poke
Appetizer · Serves 8 · 15 min, no cook

Traditional poke is spare: fish, salt, seaweed, onion, kukui nut. Everything else is a later addition. Dress it no more than an hour before serving.

  • 1½ lb sushi-grade ahi tuna, in ¾-inch cubes
  • ½ sweet onion, thinly sliced · 4 scallions, sliced
  • 3 tbsp shoyu · 1 tbsp sesame oil
  • 1 tsp coarse Hawaiian salt
  • 2 tbsp limu or crumbled nori
  • ¼ cup macadamia nuts, crushed · chilli flakes
  1. Keep the tuna very cold and cut it with a sharp knife into clean cubes.
  2. Fold everything together gently — poke should be tossed, not stirred.
  3. Rest 20 to 60 minutes refrigerated. Beyond that the shoyu starts curing the fish and the texture goes firm.
Grilled Spam Musubi
Appetizer · Makes 12 · 20 min
  • 2 cans Spam, each cut into 6 slices
  • ¼ cup shoyu · 3 tbsp sugar · 1 tbsp mirin
  • 4 cups cooked short-grain rice, still warm
  • 4 sheets nori, cut into thirds lengthwise
  • Furikake
  1. Grill the Spam slices over direct heat, 2 to 3 minutes a side, until browned and crisp at the edges.
  2. Simmer the shoyu, sugar and mirin into a glaze in a small pan and turn the grilled slices through it.
  3. Lay a nori strip down, set the empty Spam can (or a musubi mould) on it, press in rice about ¾ inch thick, dust with furikake, top with a slice, and press.
  4. Lift the mould away and wrap the nori around, sealing the end with a dab of water.
  5. This will convert everyone who says they don't like Spam.
Kalua Pork
Main · Serves 8 with leftovers · 8 hr

Three ingredients. The banana leaves are what make it kalua rather than ordinary pulled pork — they trap steam against the meat so it ends up somewhere between smoked and steamed.

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 6 lb
  • 3 tbsp coarse Hawaiian sea salt
  • Banana leaves, thawed and passed briefly over the fire to soften
  • Kiawe or mesquite chunks
  • Hawaiian sweet rolls (Chapter 19), to serve
  1. Stab the shoulder all over and rub the salt in hard. Do this the day before.
  2. Wrap the shoulder completely in two or three layers of softened banana leaf and tie with twine, leaving the top a little open so smoke can reach it for the first hours.
  3. Deflector in, water pan beneath, kettle at 275°F. Pork on, wood on the coals for the first two hours.
  4. After hour three, close the leaf wrapping over the top completely.
  5. 7 to 8 hours to 200–205°F. Rest, still wrapped, for 45 minutes.
  6. Unwrap over a tray so you catch the juices. Shred and toss the meat back through those juices — that liquid is most of the flavour.
  7. Pile onto split sweet rolls for sliders.
Huli Huli Chicken
Main · Serves 8 · 90 min
  • 2 whole chickens, about 4 lb each
  • Huli huli sauce (Chapter 24), divided — half for marinating, half for glazing
  1. Marinate the chickens overnight in half the sauce. Pat dry.
  2. Truss tightly, spit both birds facing opposite ways, balance the load.
  3. Indirect at 350°F with a drip pan below. 75 to 90 minutes to 165°F in the thigh.
  4. Glaze only in the final 20 minutes, brushing on three or four thin coats and letting each set. The sauce is more than a quarter sugar and will blacken if it goes on earlier.
  5. Rest 15 minutes, then cut into quarters. Huli means turn — which is what the spit has been doing all afternoon.
Lomi Lomi Salmon & Mac Salad
Sides · Serves 8 · No cook
  • Lomi lomi
  • 1 lb salted salmon, soaked in cold water 2 hours and diced
  • 6 tomatoes, diced · 1 sweet onion, finely diced · 4 scallions
  • Ice water · coarse salt
  • Mac salad
  • 1 lb elbow macaroni, cooked soft
  • 1½ cups mayonnaise · ¼ cup milk · 2 tbsp cider vinegar
  • 1 carrot, grated · ½ onion, grated · 1 tsp sugar · salt and pepper
  1. Lomi lomi means to massage — work the salmon, tomato and onion together with your hands rather than stirring. Chill hard and serve cold.
  2. For the mac salad, cook the macaroni past al dente, drain, and toss with the vinegar while hot so it absorbs.

Butter Mochi & Haupia
Dessert · Serves 8 · 1 hr

Butter mochi is the ideal grill dessert: chewy, dense, and nearly impossible to overbake. Mochiko — sweet rice flour — is not interchangeable with rice flour or any other flour, and the dish does not work without it.

  • Butter mochi
  • 1 lb (3 cups) mochiko sweet rice flour
  • 2 cups sugar · 2 tsp baking powder
  • 1 can (13.5 oz) coconut milk · 1 can (12 oz) evaporated milk
  • 5 eggs · 1 stick butter, melted · 2 tsp vanilla
  • Shredded coconut, for the top
  • Haupia
  • 2 cans coconut milk · ½ cup sugar
  • 6 tbsp cornstarch · ½ cup water · pinch of salt
  1. Haupia, a day ahead: whisk the cornstarch into the water. Heat the coconut milk, sugar and salt, then whisk in the slurry and cook, stirring constantly, until very thick — 5 to 8 minutes. Pour into a lined 8-inch pan and chill until firm, then cut into squares.
  2. Butter mochi: whisk the mochiko, sugar and baking powder. Beat in the eggs, then both milks, the butter and vanilla, to a smooth, thin batter.
  3. Pour into a buttered 12-inch cast-iron skillet and scatter coconut over.
  4. Bake over the deflector at 350°F for 50 to 60 minutes, rotating halfway, until the top is golden and slightly crusted and the centre is set but still yielding.
  5. Cool at least 30 minutes before cutting — hot butter mochi is gummy and cooled butter mochi is perfect.
