FIRE & KETTLE
Thirty-four menus for eight people, cooked on no more than two Weber kettles.

Every dish in this book is cooked over charcoal or wood on a 22-inch kettle grill. No oven. No stovetop. No gas. The only permitted appliances are the two kettles themselves and three accessories: a heat deflector, a rotisserie, and a pizza stone.

Temperatures are Fahrenheit. Every menu serves eight. Meat is done when the thermometer says so, not when the clock does.

Part One and Part Two are about fire. Part Three is the library of doughs, sauces, rubs and marinades that the menus draw on. Parts Four through Seven are the menus themselves.

Front Matter

How to Use This Book

Three rules govern everything here: two kettles maximum, fire as the only heat source, and eight people at the table.

Every menu chapter is built in the same order, and the order is the order you should read it in. Concept tells you what the meal is and when to cook it. The Menu lists the dishes. Grill Plan & Fuel assigns each kettle a job and tells you what to burn — read this before you shop, because it determines how much charcoal and which wood you need. Shopping List is scaled for eight and grouped by where you'll find things in a store. Prep Schedule is a countdown from the moment food hits the table. Then the recipes, then the drinks.

What the icons in the margin mean

Make Ahead

This can be done a day or more in advance, and in most cases should be. Nearly every menu in this book is survivable only because of what you did yesterday.

Note

A technique detail, a substitution, or a piece of context that will make the dish better.

Watch Out

The specific way this dish is usually ruined.

On serving eight

Every quantity assumes eight adults eating a full meal with appetizers and dessert, with modest leftovers. Plan on roughly ½ to ¾ pound of cooked meat per person for a centerpiece protein, which after bone, fat and shrinkage usually means 1 to 1¼ pounds raw. To scale down to four, halve the proteins and produce but not the sauces, rubs, or doughs — those are fiddly in small batches and they keep. To scale up to sixteen, double everything and add a third of your charcoal estimate again, because you will be cooking in batches and the fire has to live longer.

On temperature and time

Every cooking time in this book is a planning aid. Every temperature is the actual instruction. A brisket is not done at eleven hours; it is done at 203°F. Chicken is not done when the timer rings; it is done at 165°F in the thigh. Charcoal cooking has too many variables — wind, humidity, fuel quality, how often you lifted the lid — for times to be anything but estimates. Buy a thermometer before you buy anything else in this book.

What you genuinely need

  • Two 22-inch kettle grills — though several of the twenty-four run on one.
  • A chimney starter. Two is better.
  • An instant-read thermometer, and ideally a leave-in probe.
  • A 12-inch cast-iron skillet and a 6-quart camp Dutch oven with a flat lid.
  • Charcoal baskets or fire bricks for banking coals.
  • Long tongs, a grill brush, heavy gloves, and a full chimney's worth of patience.

And the three accessories this book is built around: a heat deflector, a rotisserie, and a pizza stone. Chapter 5 covers all three.

If you are cooking for the first time from this book

Start with Hanoi Bún Chả or Pacific Northwest. Both run on one kettle, both take about an hour of active cooking, and neither punishes a fire that isn't perfectly behaved. Then move to Pollo a la Brasa to learn the rotisserie, and Santa Maria Style to learn two-zone beef. Save Punjabi Tandoori and Island Style Luau until you've run a deflector fire at least twice.

Part One

Fire

A kettle grill is not one appliance. It is an oven, a smoker, a hearth, and a steamer, and the difference between them is entirely in how you arrange sixty pieces of burning carbon and how far you open two holes.

Part One · Fire

The Kettle, Four Ways

Everything in this book is a variation on four uses of the same piece of enamelled steel.

The hearth

Coals spread under an open or briefly closed grate, food directly above, cooking in minutes. This is the oldest and least forgiving mode: skirt steak, yakitori, bún chả pork, oysters, whole fish. The fire is at its hottest and shortest-lived, you stand over it the whole time, and the only real skill is knowing when to move something off. A hearth fire has no reserve — when it's spent, it's spent, which is why a second lit chimney standing by is not optional for these menus.

The oven

Coals banked to one side or ringed around the edge, a deflector or a drip pan blocking the direct radiant heat, food in the shadow, lid down. The kettle now behaves like a convection oven running anywhere from 275°F to 500°F depending on how much fuel is burning and how open the vents are. This is how you bake buns, biscuits, hand pies, crostata, butter mochi and cobbler; how you roast a lamb shoulder; how you hold a Dutch oven of jambalaya. Anything you would put in a home oven, you can put here.

The smoker

The oven, run cold — 225°F to 300°F — for six hours or more, with wood added in the first third. The trick is not the smoke, it's the endurance: a fire arranged so that it burns only a little of itself at a time. That's what the snake method is for, and it's covered in Chapter 3.

The steamer

A full, hot fire under a pan of liquid with the lid down. The kettle becomes a sealed steam box, which is how the Down East Clambake works, how sticky rice and banana leaf packets cook, and how a beer-and-onion bath holds sixteen bratwurst hot for an hour without drying them out. Almost nobody uses a kettle this way and it is one of its best tricks.

The one thing to internalise

The lid is not a cover. The lid is the second half of the cooking system. With the lid off, a kettle is a radiant grill and the only heat is coming from below. With the lid on, air is drawn through the bottom vent, across the coals, over the food, and out the top vent — and the whole dome radiates heat back down. Most people who say "my grill can't hold temperature" are opening it every four minutes.

Part One · Fire

Fuel and Fire Math

How much charcoal to buy, what kind, and how long it will actually last.

Briquettes or lump

Briquettes are compressed sawdust and binders, manufactured to a uniform size. They burn cooler, longer, and above all predictably — a briquette is the same as the briquette next to it, which is what makes the snake method possible. They leave more ash. Use them for anything that has to hold a temperature for hours, and for cold-weather cooking, where predictability matters most.

Lump charcoal is carbonised hardwood. It lights faster, burns hotter, produces less ash, and tastes cleaner. It is also irregular — a bag contains fist-sized chunks and gravel — so it burns unevenly and unpredictably. Use it for hearth fires, high-heat searing, pizza-stone work, and anything where you want maximum temperature in a hurry.

Most menus in this book specify one or the other. Where it says lump and you have briquettes, you will be fine; you'll just want more of them and a longer preheat.

The chimney is the unit of measurement

A standard chimney starter holds roughly 4 pounds, or about 100 briquettes. Every fuel estimate in this book is expressed in chimneys, because weighing charcoal is absurd and counting it is worse. A rough sense of what a chimney does:

FireFuelTemperatureUseful life
Full chimney, spread wide, lid off1 chimney600°F+ at the grate30–45 min
Full chimney, spread, lid on1 chimney450–550°F1–2 hr
Half chimney, banked, lid on½ chimney325–375°F1–1½ hr

How much to buy

An 18-pound bag of briquettes is about 4½ chimneys. A menu marked "moderate" in this book typically burns 3 to 4 chimneys across two kettles; an all-day menu like Jamaican Jerk or Island Style Luau will burn 6 to 7. Buy two bags for any all-day cook and never plan to finish a bag exactly — running out of fuel at hour six of a brisket is the single most common way a cook fails, and it is entirely preventable for nine dollars.

Lighting

A chimney starter with two sheets of crumpled newspaper or a paraffin cube underneath is lit in 15 to 20 minutes, and it's ready when the top coals are ashed grey and you can see flame at the rim. Never use lighter fluid; it flavours the food and it flavours it badly. For long cooks, you need only 8 to 12 lit briquettes to start a snake — the whole point is that the rest of the fuel is cold.

Keep a reserve

For every hearth-fire menu in this book — yakitori, bún chả, Aegean fish, char-grilled oysters — light a second chimney about forty minutes in and leave it standing on a safe surface. When the main bed starts to grey out mid-service, you top up and keep going instead of apologising to eight people.

Part One · Fire

The Six Fires

Every menu in this book uses one or more of these six arrangements. Learn them once.

1 · Direct, spread

A full chimney dumped and raked into an even layer across the whole charcoal grate. Maximum heat, maximum area, no escape route. Use for: oysters, whole fish, skewers in volume, flatbread on the grate. Menus: Yakitori, Aegean, Hanoi, New Orleans.

2 · Two-zone

The same chimney raked onto one half or two-thirds of the grate, leaving the rest bare. You now have a searing zone and a parking zone, and you can move anything that's browning too fast. This is the default fire for most cooking and the single most useful arrangement there is. Menus: Asado, Santa Maria, Taquiza, Pacific Northwest, Wisconsin.

3 · Banked with a deflector

Coals pushed to one side or into a ring around the perimeter, the heat deflector set over them, food above and beyond. Now you have an oven. Vent control sets the temperature: wide open gives you 400–450°F for baking; a third open gives you 300–325°F for roasting and braising. Menus: everything with bread, beans, jambalaya, dal, or a Dutch oven in it.

4 · The snake

Briquettes laid in a continuous overlapping line around three-quarters of the perimeter of the charcoal grate — two wide and two high, like a row of dominoes fallen against each other. Roughly 60 briquettes. Wood chunks sit on the first third. A water pan goes in the middle. You light 8 to 12 briquettes and place them at one end, and the fire crawls slowly around the ring, burning only a handful of coals at a time.

A well-built snake holds 250 to 300°F for six hours or more with no refuelling at all. It is the reason a $150 kettle can cook a brisket as well as a $2,000 smoker. Menus: Fourth of July, Jamaican Jerk, Moroccan Mechoui.

5 · The vortex

A cone-shaped basket sitting in the centre of the charcoal grate, filled with coals, with food arranged in a ring around the outside. The shape forces a column of extremely hot air up the middle and back down the dome, which crisps skin like nothing else. Rotate the lid a quarter turn every ten minutes so the vent — and therefore the hot draw — moves around the circle. Menus: Fourth of July wings.

6 · Split baskets (rotisserie)

Coals divided into two baskets, one on the left and one on the right, with a drip pan in the empty channel between them and the spit turning above it. Nothing sits directly over fire, so nothing scorches, and the fat that renders falls into the pan rather than onto the coals. Feed each basket 8 to 10 fresh briquettes every 45 minutes. Menus: Tuscan, Pollo a la Brasa, Alsatian, Luau, Taquiza.

Building a snake that actually works

The two failure modes are both fatal and both avoidable. If the briquettes are spaced apart, the fire goes out. If the line doubles back on itself or the two ends touch, the fire jumps the gap, both ends burn at once, and your 250°F becomes 400°F in twenty minutes. Leave a clear gap of at least three inches between the head and the tail of the snake, and make sure every briquette is touching its neighbours.

Part One · Fire

Vents, Temperature, and What Went Wrong

Two holes control a fire. One of them matters much more than the other.

The bottom vent controls how much oxygen reaches the coals, and therefore how fast they burn and how hot the fire gets. It is the throttle. The top vent controls how fast air leaves, and therefore how strongly air is drawn through the whole system; it is a fine adjustment and it should almost always stay at least partly open, because closing it traps stale, acrid smoke against your food.

Position the top vent opposite the coals, above the food. That forces the heat and smoke to travel across the cooking chamber to get out, rather than rising straight up from the fire and escaping.

Starting positions

TargetBottom ventTop ventUsed for
225–250°F¼ open½ openBrisket, pork shoulder, jerk pork
275–300°F⅓ open½–¾ openRibs, lamb shoulder, kalua pork
325–375°F½ openFullRotisserie, roasting, braising
400–450°F¾ openFullBaking bread, pies, cobbler
500°F+FullFullPizza stone, flatbread, searing

Adjusting

Vents respond slowly — give any change fifteen to twenty minutes before you judge it. The most common error is chasing the temperature: closing the vent, seeing no change in four minutes, closing it further, and then twenty minutes later being forty degrees too cold with a smothered fire that's hard to revive. Make one small change and wait.

It is always easier to bring a fire up than to bring it down. Start low and open up.

Where to measure

The thermometer in a kettle lid reads the air at the top of the dome, which can be 25 to 50 degrees off from the air at the grate where your food actually is. If a recipe in this book says 275°F, it means at the grate. A probe thermometer with its tip clipped an inch above the cooking surface is worth more than any other upgrade you can make.

Part One · Fire

The Three Accessories

A deflector, a rotisserie, and a stone. Between them they turn a grill into an oven, a spit-roaster, and a tandoor.

The heat deflector

A ceramic or steel plate that sits between the coals and the food, blocking radiant heat and forcing everything to cook by convection instead. This is the accessory that makes the largest difference to what a kettle can do, because it converts a grill into a genuine oven — stable, even, and capable of baking bread without scorching the bottom.

Setting it up: bank or ring your coals, set the deflector over them, close the lid and let the whole system come to temperature for 15 minutes before food goes in. The deflector itself needs to heat up; putting a Dutch oven onto a cold plate gives you a cold bottom for half an hour.

What it doesn't do: it does not let you skip vent control. The deflector shapes the heat; the vents still decide how much there is.

No deflector?

A heavy-gauge disposable foil pan filled with water, set on the charcoal grate beside the coals, does roughly 80 percent of the job — it blocks radiant heat, adds humidity, and catches drips. Two fire bricks laid over the coals work too. Neither is as even, but neither will ruin a cook.

The rotisserie

A motorised spit that turns food continuously above an indirect fire. It self-bastes — fat that renders runs around the surface instead of dripping away — and it browns with a completeness nothing stationary can match. It is also the least demanding way to cook: once it's spinning, you leave it alone for two hours.

Balance is everything. Before you light anything, load the spit, tighten the forks hard, and rest the bar across two glasses. If it rolls heavily to one side, reposition or re-truss. An unbalanced load thumps, cooks unevenly, and will stall a cheap motor.

Always run it indirect, with split baskets and a drip pan beneath. Anything sitting over live coals will scorch on every rotation.

Truss tightly. Loose wings, legs and flaps catch, tear, and burn. Twine is not optional.

Glaze late. Any sauce containing sugar — huli huli, tare, al pastor adobo — goes on in the final 15 to 20 minutes only. Earlier and it carbonises long before the interior is done.

The pizza stone

A slab of cordierite or ceramic that stores an enormous amount of heat and gives it back fast, which is what puffs a naan, blisters a tarte flambée in ninety seconds, and gives a hand pie a crisp bottom instead of a pale one.

The stone goes in cold

Put the stone on the cooking grate before you light the kettle, or at the latest while the coals are still spreading, and let it come up with the fire for a full 30 minutes. Sliding a cold stone onto a hot grate is the only reliable way to crack one. Never wash it with soap, never oil it, and let it cool completely in the closed grill.

For flatbread, set the stone directly over a full spread of lump and close the lid: you are aiming for a stone surface of 600 to 700°F, which is genuine tandoor territory. For baking pastry, set the stone on the indirect side above a deflector and treat it as an oven floor at 375 to 425°F — it evens out the bottom heat and stops a Dutch oven or a pie tin from scorching.

Part One · Fire

Wood

Smoke is a seasoning. Like salt, more is not better, and the wrong one is worse than none.

Two or three fist-sized chunks is the right dose for a whole cook. Chips burn out in fifteen minutes and are mostly useful for short cooks; chunks smoulder for an hour or more and are what this book assumes. Add all your wood in the first third of a cook — meat stops taking on smoke in any meaningful way once its surface dries and the bark sets, and wood added late just makes acrid smoke that sits on the food.

Soaking wood chips is pointless. Wet wood produces steam, then smoke; you've delayed the smoke, not improved it. Use dry chunks and control the dose instead.

Never burn

Pine, fir, spruce, cedar as fuel, eucalyptus, elm, sycamore, and anything painted, treated, stained, or salvaged from a pallet. Resinous softwoods coat food in soot and turpentine flavours. Cedar is used in this book as a cooking surface — a soaked plank — which is a completely different thing from burning it.

When not to smoke at all

Several menus here deliberately use little or no wood. Yakitori and pollo a la brasa are roasts, not barbecue — Japanese grilling in particular is built around tasting the ingredient, and heavy smoke is a fault, not a feature. Tandoori food comes out of a clean-burning clay oven; there's no wood smoke in a real tandoor at all. And on delicate white fish, more than a whisper of smoke simply buries it.

Dhungar — smoke without a fire

An Indian technique for adding smoke to something already cooked. Set a small metal or foil bowl in the middle of a pot of dal, curry, or marinade. Drop in one glowing ember, pour a teaspoon of ghee over it, and clamp the lid on immediately. Two minutes later you have a deeply smoky dish and no wood was involved. Used in the Punjabi Tandoori menu.

The Rest of the Hardware

Beyond the three accessories, six things do almost all the remaining work.

The cast-iron skillet

A 12-inch skillet turns the kettle into a stovetop. It sears, it fries, it holds, it bakes. Provoleta, queso fundido, maque choux, wild mushrooms, cherry sauce, butter mochi, gajar halwa, German potato salad and bananas Foster in this book are all skillet dishes. Cast iron on a grill gets hotter than it ever does indoors — use gloves, and treat a preheated pan as a genuinely dangerous object.

The Dutch oven

A 6-quart camp Dutch oven with legs and a flat, rimmed lid is the single most versatile pot you can own for this kind of cooking. Flat lid means you can pile coals on top for heat from above; legs mean it sits stably over a fire. Used here for jambalaya, rice and peas, pinquito beans, choucroute, dal makhani, blueberry grunt and cobbler.

The plancha or griddle

A flat steel plate over direct coals. Necessary for anything that would fall through a grate or needs full surface contact: butter burgers, smash patties, griddled bun faces. A cast-iron griddle pan or even a heavy sheet of steel works.

Fish baskets and grill baskets

A hinged basket is the difference between serving a whole branzino and serving half a branzino stuck to the grate and half on the plate. Oil the basket, oil the fish, preheat the basket with the grill. For vegetables, a perforated basket saves an enormous amount of tong work.

Disposable foil pans

Unglamorous and indispensable. They are your drip pan under a rotisserie, your water pan in a snake, your beer bath for brats, your steaming vessel for a clambake, and your holding vessel for anything that needs to stay warm at the edge of the fire. Buy the heavy-gauge ones; the thin ones buckle when you lift them full.

Planks

Untreated cedar, alder or oak planks, soaked at least two hours, give food a gentle wood flavour and a protective barrier from direct heat. Keep a spray bottle of water to hand — the edges will catch, and that's fine as long as it doesn't spread. Never use construction lumber, which is chemically treated.

The grate itself

Clean it while it's hot, not cold: burn off residue with the lid on for ten minutes, then brush hard. Oil the food, not the grate — oil on a hot grate just polymerises into varnish and smokes. The exception is fish, where you oil both and pray.

Part One · Fire

Cold, Wind, and Rain

Four of the eighteen menus are winter menus. Charcoal works in the cold; you just have to feed it more.

Cold

Below about 40°F, a kettle loses heat through its walls faster than it makes it, and the food itself starts colder. Budget 25 to 30 percent more fuel and expect cooking times to run 20 to 30 percent longer. Use briquettes rather than lump — the steadier burn matters more when conditions are working against you. Bring meat closer to room temperature before it goes on, and be especially disciplined about not lifting the lid.

Wind

Wind is worse than cold. It strips heat from the dome and, more importantly, it blows through the bottom vent and makes the fire burn hard and unevenly — you'll see temperature spikes you can't explain. Position the kettle so a wall, fence or car blocks the prevailing wind, and turn the grill so the bottom vent faces away from it. Never improvise a windbreak out of anything flammable, and never bring a kettle into a garage, tent, or any enclosed space: charcoal produces carbon monoxide, and it kills people every year.

Rain

Light rain is a non-event — the lid is a roof. Heavy rain cools the dome dramatically; add fuel and accept a longer cook. Keep the charcoal bag under cover, because damp briquettes are the most common cause of a fire that simply will not climb past 300°F.

Altitude

Above about 3,000 feet, water boils cooler and things take longer; anything braised or steamed — jambalaya, beans, the clambake — needs extra time. Baked goods rise faster and can collapse: reduce leavening slightly and expect a shorter bake.

Thermometers and Doneness

The only instrument in this book that cannot be improvised.

Buy two things: an instant-read thermometer for spot checks, and a leave-in probe with a cable that runs out under the lid so you can watch a long cook without opening it. Together they cost less than the brisket they will save.

Measure in the thickest part, away from bone. For poultry, the thigh, not the breast. For a whole fish, the thickest point behind the head. For a rotisserie roast, stop the motor and probe the centre.

These are the temperatures at which food comes off the fire, not the temperature it finishes at. A large roast carries over 5 to 10 degrees while it rests.

The stall

Somewhere around 150 to 165°F, a large cut of meat will stop rising in temperature and stay there for hours. This is evaporative cooling, it is normal, and it is not a broken fire. Your options are to wait it out or to wrap the meat in foil or butcher paper, which stops the evaporation and pushes through. Do not raise the heat in a panic.

Flare-ups, Safety, and the Long Rest

How not to burn the food, the house, or your guests.

Flare-ups

A flare-up is fat igniting on the coals. The fix is never water — water blasts ash onto the food and can crack the enamel. The fix is to move the food to the cool zone and let the flame burn itself out, then move it back. If flare-ups are constant, you have too much fat dripping onto too hot a fire: switch to two-zone, or slide a drip pan under the food.

Carbon monoxide

Charcoal produces carbon monoxide as it burns, and it is odourless. Never use a kettle indoors, in a garage, on an enclosed porch, in a tent, or anywhere without free-moving air. This is the one instruction in this book with a fatal failure mode.

Ash and disposal

Coals stay hot for many hours after they look dead. Close both vents completely to smother a finished fire, leave the kettle closed overnight, and only then empty the ash catcher — into a metal container, never a plastic bin or a paper bag.

Food safety in the open

Marinades that touched raw meat are not sauces. If you want to serve a marinade, reserve a portion before the meat goes in. Never reuse a basting brush between raw and cooked. Keep raw proteins in a cooler, not on a table in the sun, and use separate boards and platters for raw and cooked — the single most common mistake at a backyard cook is carrying finished meat back to the plate it arrived on.

The long rest

A brisket, pork shoulder, lamb shoulder or kalua pork improves dramatically with a long rest, and it also buys you an enormous amount of scheduling freedom. Wrap the finished meat in foil, then in two old towels, and put the bundle in an empty cooler with the lid closed. It will hold above 140°F for two to four hours, the juices will redistribute, and connective tissue will keep softening.

This is the professional secret of every all-day menu in this book: finish the centrepiece early, park it in the cooler, and spend the last two hours cooking things that are actually fun to cook while your guests watch.

Holding

Other holds worth knowing: sausages live happily for an hour in a beer-and-onion bath at the edge of the fire; flatbreads stack in a clean tea towel inside a covered dish and stay soft; fried or grilled items hold crisp on a rack over the cool zone with the lid off. Nothing holds well in a sealed container, which turns crisp into soggy in ten minutes.
