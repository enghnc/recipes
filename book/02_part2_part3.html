The Rotisserie

Used in five menus: Tuscan porchetta, pollo a la brasa, Alsatian capon, Hawaiian huli huli chicken, and the Mexican trompo.

The method, start to finish

  1. Truss first, light second. Tie wings to the body, tie legs together, and tie any rolled roast at one-inch intervals. Every loose flap will catch on the ring, tear, and burn.
  2. Load and balance. Push the spit through the centre of mass, seat both forks, and tighten the thumbscrews as hard as your hands allow. Rest the loaded bar across two glasses and give it a gentle spin. If it falls heavily to one side, reposition. Two minutes here saves a ruined roast.
  3. Build split baskets. One chimney of lit coals divided between two baskets, left and right, with a disposable drip pan in the channel between them. Nothing may sit directly over fire.
  4. Run at 325 to 375°F. Bottom vent about half open, top vent full. Let the kettle stabilise for ten minutes before the spit goes on.
  5. Feed the fire. Add 8 to 10 unlit briquettes to each basket every 45 minutes. They light off the existing bed. For cooks over two hours, expect to do this two or three times.
  6. Baste on a schedule, glaze at the end. Fat or butter bastes can go on every 30 minutes. Anything with sugar in it — huli huli, tare, achiote adobo, maple — goes on only in the final 15 to 20 minutes.
  7. Finish hot if you want crackling. For porchetta or any skin-on roast, pull the drip pan, rake both baskets into one pile directly under the meat, take the lid off, and let the skin blister for the last 10 to 15 minutes while it keeps turning.
  8. Stop the motor to probe. Then rest, on the spit or off, for 15 to 20 minutes.

Baking on a Kettle

Buns, biscuits, hand pies, crostata, cobbler, butter mochi. A deflector plus a stable fire is an oven, and it behaves like one.

The standard baking fire

  1. Light half to two-thirds of a chimney. Bank the coals to one side or ring them around the edge.
  2. Set the heat deflector over the coals. If you also have the pizza stone, set it on the cooking grate on the indirect side — it acts as an oven floor and evens out the bottom heat.
  3. Close the lid, top vent above the empty side, and let it stabilise for 15 minutes.
  4. Target 350°F for enriched breads, 375–400°F for biscuits, pies and cobblers, 325°F for anything custardy.
  5. Bake in cast iron, a foil pan, or a Dutch oven — metal is much more forgiving on a grill than glass or ceramic, which can shock and crack.
  6. Rotate 180° halfway. A kettle is never perfectly even. This one habit fixes almost all of it.
  7. Judge by colour and internal temperature, not the clock. Enriched bread is done at 190–200°F internal.

The Dutch oven, heated from both sides

For cobblers, grunts and anything you want browned on top, put the Dutch oven on the indirect side and shovel 8 to 12 lit coals onto the flat lid. You now have top and bottom heat, which is the only way to brown a crust that isn't facing the fire. Rotate the pot a third-turn and the lid a third-turn in the opposite direction every 10 to 15 minutes so no hot spot sits still.

Why grill-baked bread is good

A kettle runs more humid than a home oven — there's moisture coming off the fuel and the food — which is exactly what enriched doughs want. Buns baked on a kettle tend to have a softer crust and better oven spring than the same dough baked indoors. It is not a compromise.

The Stone as a Tandoor

Naan, pita, khobz, schiacciata, tarte flambée. Two minutes each, and the whole technique is heat.

A tandoor works because a clay wall holding 700°F transfers heat into dough faster than the dough can lose moisture — the water inside flashes to steam and the bread balloons before the surface can set. A pizza stone on a kettle does the same thing, and it is the closest most people will get to a tandoor without building one.

The method

  1. Stone on the grate before you light anything. Cold stone, cold grill, together.
  2. A full chimney of lump, spread edge to edge directly beneath the stone. Lid on, both vents wide.
  3. Preheat 30 to 40 minutes. You are aiming for a stone surface of 600 to 700°F. Test it by flicking a few drops of water: they should skitter and vanish instantly.
  4. Stretch the dough thin — thinner than feels right — and slap it straight onto the stone. No pan, no paper.
  5. Lid down. 60 to 90 seconds for naan, 2 to 3 minutes for a thicker flatbread, 2 to 4 minutes for tarte flambée. It's done when the top has puffed and the edges show dark blisters.
  6. Off with a peel or long tongs, brushed with ghee, butter or oil the second it lands.
  7. Stack finished breads in a clean tea towel inside a covered bowl. They steam each other soft.
Three ways this goes wrong

Stone not hot enough: the bread sits, dries, and turns into a cracker instead of puffing. Wait longer. Dough too thick: it browns outside and stays raw inside. Working too slowly: the stone drops about 25°F per bread. Give it two or three minutes to recover every four breads, or run the fire hotter than you think you need.

If you have no stone, a heavy cast-iron skillet or griddle preheated the same way is a workable substitute — smaller, but it holds heat well and recovers fast.

Low and Slow

Brisket, ribs, jerk pork, lamb shoulder, kalua pork. Six to nine hours, and most of it is waiting.

What is actually happening

Tough cuts are tough because they are full of collagen. Collagen begins converting to gelatin around 160°F and does it properly somewhere between 195 and 205°F — but the conversion takes time as well as temperature, which is why you cannot rush a shoulder by cooking it hotter. Meanwhile the surface dries and, in the presence of smoke and rendering fat, forms bark: a dark, dense, intensely flavoured crust that is the entire point.

The sequence

  1. Season the night before. Salt needs hours to penetrate. Rub goes on 8 to 24 hours ahead, meat sits uncovered in the fridge so the surface dries.
  2. Build a snake (Chapter 3), wood chunks on the first third, water pan in the middle. Light 8 to 12 briquettes and place them at one end.
  3. Meat on, fat cap up unless the recipe says otherwise, positioned over the water pan and away from the burning end.
  4. Do not open the lid for the first two hours. Nothing useful is happening that you can see.
  5. Spritz from hour three if the bark looks dry — apple juice, cider vinegar and water. Every 45 minutes at most.
  6. The stall arrives at 150 to 165°F. Either wait it out, or wrap: foil for speed and tenderness, butcher paper for a better bark. Wrapping typically cuts two hours.
  7. Pull by feel and temperature. A probe should slide in like it's going into room-temperature butter. Numbers in Chapter 9.
  8. Rest long. Foil, towels, cooler, one to two hours minimum for a brisket.

Ribs: the 3-2-1 frame

For St. Louis-cut spares at 275°F: 3 hours naked on the grate, 2 hours wrapped in foil with a little liquid, 1 hour unwrapped with sauce to set. Baby backs want less — roughly 2-2-1. Treat these as starting proportions, not law; the real test is the bend test, where you lift a rack at one end with tongs and it cracks on the surface and bends to about a 90-degree angle without falling apart.

On bark

Sugar in a rub caramelises and then burns; salt and pepper do not. That's why Central Texas rubs are salt and pepper only, and why sweet rubs belong on things cooked below 275°F or sauced at the end. If your bark tastes bitter rather than dark, there was too much sugar or too much wood.

Fish and Shellfish over Live Fire

Aegean branzino, planked salmon, char-grilled oysters, clams, Dungeness crab, miso mackerel.

Why fish sticks, and how to stop it

Protein bonds to bare metal. The defence is three-part and all three parts are required: a scrupulously clean grate, a hot grate, and oil on the fish rather than on the grate. Then — and this is the part people fail — leave it alone. Fish releases itself when a crust has formed. If it resists when you try to turn it, it is not ready; wait another minute and it will lift cleanly.

Whole fish

Score the flesh to the bone twice on each side so heat penetrates evenly and the seasoning gets in. Stuff the cavity with lemon, herbs, garlic. Use a hinged fish basket, oiled and preheated with the grill. Over a hot direct fire, a 1-pound branzino or bream takes 6 to 8 minutes a side. It's done at 135–140°F, or when the flesh at the thickest point parts from the bone.

Planks

Soak an untreated cedar or alder plank for at least two hours. Set the fish on the plank, plank on the indirect side, lid down at 350°F. A whole side of salmon takes 20 to 30 minutes and comes off at 125 to 130°F. The plank means no sticking, no turning, and a gentle smoke. Keep a spray bottle handy for edges that catch.

Shellfish

  • Oysters on the half shell: shucked, topped with compound butter, straight onto a very hot direct fire. 5 to 7 minutes, off when the butter is boiling hard and the edges of the oyster ruffle.
  • Clams and mussels in the shell: straight on the grate over high heat. They open in 4 to 8 minutes. Anything still shut after the rest have opened goes in the bin.
  • Shrimp: shell-on is better — the shell protects the meat and carries flavour. 2 to 3 minutes a side.
  • Crab: cook it first (steamed or bought cooked), then split it, brush with butter, and char the cut face for 4 to 5 minutes for flavour only.
  • Octopus: must be braised until tender before it ever sees the grill. Then 2 to 3 minutes a side over a fierce fire for char.
The one rule for fish

Pull it before you think it's ready. Carryover cooking is brutal on fish, and there is no recovering from overcooked salmon. If it flakes easily in the pan, it was already overcooked two minutes earlier.

Skewers

Yakitori, souvlaki, anticuchos, tikka, nem nướng, brochettes. Eight menus lean on them.

  • Two skewers or flat ones. A single round skewer lets everything spin when you turn it. Use flat bamboo paddle skewers, or run two thin skewers in parallel.
  • Soak bamboo 30 minutes, and leave the handle ends off the fire — on a kettle you can rest them on the rim or on a folded strip of foil.
  • Uniform pieces, loosely threaded. Pieces jammed tight steam each other; pieces with a little air between them brown.
  • Don't mix cooking times on one skewer. Chicken and peppers do not finish together. Skewer them separately and combine on the plate.
  • Direct, hot, and turned often. Most skewers want 3 to 4 minutes a side over a strong fire, turned two or three times.
  • Glaze in the last two minutes. Tare, huli huli, adobo, anything sweet. Two or three coats, each one allowed to set.
The robata channel

For yakitori, don't spread the coals — pile lump into a narrow, deep strip down the centre of the charcoal grate. Rest the skewers across the rim so the meat sits over the channel and the handles hang in open air. This concentrates fierce heat under a narrow band, cooks small skewers in three minutes, and keeps the handles cool enough to touch.

Cast Iron and the Plancha

For everything that would fall through a grate, needs full surface contact, or has to be a sauce by the end.

Skillet work

A cast-iron pan on a live fire gets far hotter than it ever does on a domestic hob, which is an advantage for searing and a liability for anything with sugar or dairy in it. Preheat the pan over the direct zone, but do the actual cooking wherever the heat is right — you can slide a pan to the indirect side and treat it as a low burner. Always use two gloved hands and assume the handle is 400°F, because it is.

The plancha

A flat steel plate over direct coals. Preheat 10 minutes, oil lightly, and cook things that need full contact: butter burgers, smash patties, griddled bun faces, spaetzle, Spam for musubi, quesadillas. The surface should be hot enough that a drop of water balls up and runs, not hot enough that it smokes continuously.

Steam, Boil, Braise

The least-used mode of a kettle grill, and the one that makes a clambake possible.

Steaming

Set a deep disposable roasting pan or a stockpot directly on the cooking grate over a full, hot fire. Liquid in the bottom, food layered above, the pan sealed tightly with foil or its own lid, and then the kettle lid closed over the top with all vents open. You now have a sealed steam box with a very large heat source under it. This is how the clambake works: three cups of water, a bed of rockweed, potatoes and onions, sausage and corn, clams and mussels, lobsters on top, 25 to 35 minutes.

The layering principle is simple: densest and slowest at the bottom nearest the heat, quickest at the top. Shellfish always goes last so it doesn't overcook while the potatoes catch up.

The holding bath

A foil pan of simmering liquid on the indirect side is a holding station. Sausages go in for 20 minutes before they're grilled and back in afterwards, where they will sit happily for an hour without drying out. Beer, butter and sliced onion is the Wisconsin version; the same principle works for merguez, chorizo and boudin.

Braising

A Dutch oven over a deflector at 300 to 325°F is a braising oven. Brown the meat or aromatics over direct heat first, add liquid, then move to the indirect side and put the lid on. Choucroute, rice and peas, pinquito beans and dal all work this way. Check the liquid level every 45 minutes — a grill runs drier than an oven, and everything here wants a little more liquid than an indoor recipe would call for.

Foil packets

The simplest steamer of all. Edamame with salt; cabbage and carrot with butter; artichokes with lemon before they're charred; bananas split with brown sugar and rum. Heavy foil, sealed tight, on the grate or straight on the embers for 10 to 20 minutes. There is no technique to learn and the results are consistently good.

The Master Enriched Dough

Three menus in this book bake their own buns. All three use this dough, with small adjustments, shaped three different ways.

Master Enriched Bun Dough
Makes 12 buns · 25 min active · overnight or 2½ hr rise

A soft, slightly sweet, milk-and-butter dough — the family that contains brioche, potato rolls, Hawaiian bread and New England split-tops. The milk powder is not optional if you want the texture right; it adds protein and sugars that give a tender crumb and deep browning without extra liquid.

  • 4½ cups (560 g) bread flour
  • ¼ cup (30 g) nonfat dry milk powder
  • ⅓ cup (67 g) sugar
  • 2¼ tsp (7 g) instant yeast
  • 2 tsp (12 g) fine sea salt
  • 1 cup (240 ml) whole milk, lukewarm
  • 2 large eggs, plus 1 for the wash
  • 6 tbsp (85 g) unsalted butter, soft, plus more for brushing
  1. Whisk the flour, milk powder, sugar, yeast and salt together in a large bowl.
  2. Add the milk and 2 eggs. Mix to a shaggy mass, then knead 8 to 10 minutes by hand — or 6 in a mixer — until smooth and elastic.
  3. Add the soft butter a tablespoon at a time, kneading each piece in fully before the next. The dough will look broken and hopeless for a few minutes; keep going and it will come back together, glossy and slack.
  4. Cover and rise until doubled: about 1½ hours at room temperature, or overnight in the fridge. The cold overnight rise is better — more flavour, and a firm dough is far easier to shape.
  5. Shape according to one of the three variations below. Proof, covered, until visibly puffy and slow to spring back when poked — 45 to 75 minutes, longer if the dough came out of the fridge.
  6. Brush with egg wash. Bake on the kettle over a deflector at 350°F, rotating 180° halfway, for 18 to 25 minutes, until deep gold and 190–200°F inside.
  7. Brush with melted butter the moment they come off. Cool at least 20 minutes before splitting.
Make Ahead

Mix the dough the night before and refrigerate. Baked buns keep two days wrapped at room temperature, or freeze beautifully for a month — thaw and refresh for five minutes on the cool side of the grill.

Variation I · New England Split-Top Rolls for lobster rolls
Down East Clambake · Makes 10

The defining feature is flat, crustless sides that you butter and griddle. That only happens if the rolls are baked touching one another.

  1. Reduce the sugar in the master dough to 3 tablespoons.
  2. Divide into 10 pieces of about 95 g. Shape each into a tight 4-inch log.
  3. Line them up in a buttered 9×13 foil pan with the sides touching, short ends against the pan walls. They must be crowded.
  4. Proof, egg wash the tops only, and bake at 350°F for 20 to 24 minutes.
  5. Cool, then pull apart. Each roll has two soft, pale flat sides. Slit the top down the centre, not the side.
  6. To serve: butter the flat sides generously and griddle them on a cast-iron pan over direct heat until golden, about 90 seconds a side. Fill the split top.
Variation II · Brat Buns
Wisconsin Tailgate · Makes 12
  1. Use the master dough as written. Divide into 12 pieces of about 80 g.
  2. Flatten each piece, roll up tightly from the long side into a cylinder, and seal the seam. Roll under your palms to a 6-inch log with slightly tapered ends.
  3. Arrange seam-side down on a parchment-lined foil pan, leaving 1 inch between them — unlike split-tops, these want a little crust on the sides so they can hold a bratwurst and a pile of onions without collapsing.
  4. Proof, egg wash, and sprinkle with sesame or poppy seeds if you like.
  5. Bake at 350°F for 18 to 22 minutes. Split three-quarters of the way through, not all the way.
Variation III · Hawaiian Sweet Rolls
Island Style Luau · Makes 15 pull-apart rolls
  1. To the master dough, increase the sugar to ½ cup (100 g), replace ½ cup of the milk with ½ cup pineapple juice, and add ½ teaspoon ground ginger and ½ teaspoon vanilla.
  2. Divide into 15 pieces of about 65 g and roll each into a tight ball.
  3. Arrange in a buttered 12-inch cast-iron skillet, touching, in a spiral or grid.
  4. Proof until the balls have merged into one mass and look inflated. Egg wash.
  5. Bake over a deflector at 350°F for 25 to 30 minutes, rotating halfway. They're done at 190°F; if the tops are browning too fast, tent loosely with foil.
  6. Brush with butter and a little honey. Pull apart to serve, and split for kalua pork sliders.
Note

The pineapple juice must be from a can or carton, not fresh. Fresh pineapple contains bromelain, an enzyme that digests protein — it will eat the gluten in your dough and leave you with soup.

Flatbread Doughs

Four doughs, all cooked on the stone or straight on the grate. Technique is in Chapter 13.

Naan
Punjabi Tandoori · Makes 10 · 2 hr

Yogurt and a little baking powder alongside the yeast give the chew and the blistering that define naan. The dough should be noticeably softer and stickier than a bread dough — resist adding flour.

  • 4 cups (500 g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 2 tsp fine salt
  • ¾ cup (180 g) full-fat plain yogurt
  • ¾ cup (180 ml) warm water
  • 3 tbsp neutral oil or melted ghee
  • Ghee, nigella seeds, garlic and cilantro, to finish
  1. Combine the dry ingredients. Add the yogurt, water and oil and knead 6 to 8 minutes to a soft, tacky dough.
  2. Cover and rise 1½ to 2 hours, until doubled.
  3. Divide into 10 pieces. Roll each into a ball, cover, rest 20 minutes — this rest is what lets you stretch them thin without tearing.
  4. Stretch or roll each into a teardrop about 8 inches long and very thin. Brush one side lightly with water — it helps the bread grab the stone.
  5. Slap water-side down onto a stone preheated to 600–700°F. Lid down 60 to 90 seconds, until ballooned and charred in patches.
  6. Brush immediately with ghee. For garlic naan, press minced garlic and chopped cilantro into the dough before it goes on.
Pita & Khobz pocket flatbread
Aegean Seafood · Moroccan Mechoui · Makes 10
  • 4 cups (500 g) bread flour
  • 2 tsp instant yeast
  • 2 tsp fine salt
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 1½ cups (360 ml) warm water
  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  1. Mix, knead 8 minutes to a smooth, moderately firm dough. Rise 1½ hours.
  2. Divide into 10, ball, rest 20 minutes.
  3. Roll each to an even ¼-inch round. Evenness matters more than thinness — a thick spot will stop the pocket forming.
  4. Onto a stone at 600°F+, lid down. They inflate like balloons in 90 seconds to 2 minutes, then need about 30 seconds more.
  5. Wrap in a towel as they come off; they deflate and the pocket sets as they cool.
For Moroccan khobz

Replace 1 cup of the bread flour with fine semolina and roll the rounds a little thicker, about ⅜ inch. They won't pocket as dramatically but they'll have the denser, more absorbent crumb you want for mopping up mechoui and zaalouk.

Schiacciata Tuscan flatbread
Tuscan Harvest · Makes 2 large breads
  • 4 cups (500 g) bread flour
  • 1½ tsp instant yeast
  • 2 tsp fine salt
  • 1⅔ cups (400 ml) cool water
  • ¼ cup (60 ml) olive oil, plus more for the top
  • Rosemary and flaky salt
  1. Mix to a very wet dough — it should be closer to a batter than a bread dough. Do not add flour.
  2. Rise 2 hours at room temperature, folding the dough over on itself in the bowl every 30 minutes.
  3. Divide between two well-oiled 9-inch foil pans or cast-iron skillets. Dimple hard with oiled fingertips, all the way to the bottom.
  4. Rest 30 minutes. Pour a generous slick of olive oil over the top, scatter rosemary and flaky salt.
  5. Bake on the stone or over a deflector at 450°F for 20 to 25 minutes, until deeply golden and crisp at the edges.
Tarte Flambée Base pâte à flammekueche
Alsatian Christmas · Makes 6 tartes

Unyeasted, or barely yeasted — a flammekueche base is meant to be cracker-thin and crisp, not bready. This is the easiest dough in the book.

  • 3 cups (375 g) all-purpose flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • ½ tsp instant yeast (optional)
  • ¾ cup (180 ml) water
  • 3 tbsp neutral oil
  1. Mix and knead briefly, 3 to 4 minutes, to a smooth firm dough. Rest 30 minutes, covered.
  2. Divide into 6. Roll each out paper-thin into a rough oval — you should almost be able to see through it.
  3. Top sparingly (Part Five has the topping) and slide onto a stone at 550–600°F.
  4. 2 to 4 minutes, lid down, until the edges are blackened in spots and the base is crisp. Cut into strips and eat standing up, then make the next one.

Pastry, Biscuit, and Batter

Four foundations for the grill-baked desserts in Parts Four through Six.

All-Butter Pie Dough
Pacific Northwest hand pies · Makes enough for 12 hand pies or 1 double crust
  • 2½ cups (315 g) all-purpose flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 1 cup (225 g) very cold unsalted butter, cubed
  • 6–8 tbsp ice water
  • 1 tbsp cider vinegar
  1. Toss the butter through the flour, sugar and salt. Work it with your fingertips or a pastry cutter until the pieces range from pea-sized to almond-sized. Do not go further — visible butter is what makes it flaky.
  2. Combine the vinegar with 6 tablespoons of ice water and drizzle it in, tossing with a fork, until the dough holds when squeezed. Add more water a tablespoon at a time only if needed.
  3. Press into two discs, wrap, and chill at least 1 hour — overnight is better.
  4. Roll cold, fill, crimp with a fork, cut two steam vents, brush with cream and scatter coarse sugar.
  5. Chill the assembled pies for 20 minutes before baking. On a grill, where heat is uneven, cold pastry is the difference between flaky and greasy.
  6. Bake on the preheated stone over a deflector at 400°F for 20 to 25 minutes, rotating halfway.
Pasta Frolla Italian sweet pastry
Tuscan crostata · Makes one 10-inch crostata with lattice

Closer to shortbread than to pie crust: tender and sandy rather than flaky, and much more forgiving of a slightly uneven fire.

  • 2½ cups (315 g) all-purpose flour
  • ⅔ cup (135 g) sugar
  • ½ tsp fine salt
  • 10 tbsp (140 g) cold butter, cubed
  • 2 egg yolks + 1 whole egg
  • Zest of 1 lemon
  • 1 tsp vanilla
  1. Rub the butter into the flour, sugar, salt and lemon zest until it resembles coarse meal.
  2. Add the eggs and vanilla and bring together quickly — handle it as little as possible.
  3. Chill 1 hour. Press two-thirds into a 10-inch foil tart pan or skillet, up the sides.
  4. Fill with about 1½ cups of thick jam. Roll the remaining third, cut strips, and lattice the top.
  5. Bake over a deflector at 375°F for 35 to 40 minutes, rotating halfway, until the pastry is sandy gold and the jam is bubbling.
Buttermilk Biscuits
Santa Maria shortcake · Makes 10
  • 3 cups (375 g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 tbsp sugar (¼ cup for shortcake)
  • 1 tbsp baking powder
  • ½ tsp baking soda
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 10 tbsp (140 g) cold butter, grated
  • 1¼ cups (300 ml) cold buttermilk
  1. Whisk the dry ingredients. Grate in frozen butter and toss to coat.
  2. Add the buttermilk, stir just until it comes together — lumpy is correct.
  3. Pat out to 1 inch, fold in thirds like a letter, and pat out again. Repeat twice. This builds the layers.
  4. Cut with a sharp 2½-inch cutter, pressing straight down without twisting. Twisting seals the edges and stops the rise.
  5. Arrange in a cast-iron skillet with sides touching. Bake over a deflector at 400°F for 15 to 18 minutes, rotating halfway.
Grunt & Cobbler Dumpling Batter
Down East blueberry grunt · Serves 8

A grunt is a New England steamed dessert: fruit bubbling below, soft dumplings dropped on top and cooked under a lid until they puff. The name is supposedly the sound the fruit makes. It is the single most forgiving thing you can bake on a grill.

  • 2 cups (250 g) all-purpose flour
  • 3 tbsp sugar
  • 1 tbsp baking powder
  • ½ tsp salt
  • 4 tbsp (57 g) cold butter
  • ¾ cup (180 ml) whole milk or cream
  1. Rub the butter into the dry ingredients, then stir in the milk to a thick, sticky drop batter.
  2. Bring the fruit filling to a simmer in a Dutch oven on the grill.
  3. Drop the batter in eight spoonfuls over the surface, leaving gaps for steam.
  4. Cover tightly and cook over indirect heat, lid on and not lifted, for 20 to 25 minutes. The dumplings are done when a skewer comes out clean.
  5. For a browned top — a cobbler rather than a grunt — shovel 10 lit coals onto the Dutch oven lid for the last 15 minutes.

The Sauce Library

Fourteen sauces that carry most of the flavour in this book. Nearly all are make-ahead, and nearly all are better the next day.

Chimichurri
Argentine Asado · Makes 2 cups
  • 2 large bunches flat-leaf parsley, finely chopped
  • 6 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tbsp fresh oregano (or 2 tsp dried)
  • 1–2 tsp red pepper flakes
  • ½ cup red wine vinegar
  • 1 cup olive oil
  • 1½ tsp coarse salt
  1. Chop everything by hand. A blender turns chimichurri into green paste and loses the texture entirely.
  2. Combine the parsley, garlic, oregano, chilli and salt with the vinegar. Let it sit 10 minutes — the acid takes the raw edge off the garlic.
  3. Stir in the oil. Rest at least 2 hours, ideally overnight, at room temperature.
  4. Keeps a week refrigerated. Bring back to room temperature before serving or the oil will be solid.
Salsa Criolla
Argentine Asado · Makes 3 cups
  • 1 red onion, finely diced
  • 1 green and 1 red bell pepper, finely diced
  • 3 tomatoes, seeded and diced
  • ¼ cup red wine vinegar
  • ⅓ cup olive oil
  • Salt and pepper

Nước Chấm
Hanoi Bún Chả · Makes 4 cups, enough for 8 dipping bowls

The balance point of Vietnamese cooking. Taste and adjust — fish sauces vary enormously in salt, and this should end up light, bright and barely salty, not intense.

  • 1 cup fish sauce
  • 1 cup sugar
  • 2½ cups warm water
  • Juice of 4–5 limes
  • 4 cloves garlic, finely minced
  • 2–3 red chillies, thinly sliced
  • Pickles (đồ chua)
  • 1 kohlrabi or ½ daikon, thinly sliced
  • 2 carrots, thinly sliced
  • ½ cup rice vinegar, ¼ cup sugar, 1 tsp salt
  1. Dissolve the sugar in the warm water, then add the fish sauce and lime juice. It should taste like a light, sweet-sour broth you could drink.
  2. Add garlic and chilli. The garlic should float — that's the sign the balance is right.
  3. For the pickles, toss the kohlrabi and carrot with the salt, let them weep 15 minutes, squeeze dry, then steep in the vinegar and sugar for at least an hour.
  4. Serve warm, in individual bowls, with the pickles floating in them. The grilled pork goes into the bowl.

Yakitori & Robata · Makes 2 cups

Brines and Marinades

Where the flavour actually gets in. Almost all of these want to be done the night before.

The dry brine, which applies to nearly everything

For any large piece of meat or poultry that isn't going into a wet marinade: salt it at 1 teaspoon of kosher salt per pound, all over, and leave it uncovered on a rack in the fridge for 8 to 24 hours. The salt draws moisture out, dissolves in it, and is reabsorbed carrying seasoning deep into the meat; meanwhile the surface dries, which is the single biggest factor in browning and crisp skin. This one habit improves the porchetta, the capon, the pollo a la brasa and every chicken in this book.

Pollo a la Brasa Marinade
Peru · For 2 whole chickens · 12 hr minimum
  • ½ cup soy sauce
  • ⅓ cup red wine vinegar
  • ½ cup dark beer
  • 3 tbsp ají panca paste
  • 8 cloves garlic, crushed
  • 2 tbsp ground cumin
  • 1 tbsp dried oregano, 1 tbsp black pepper
  • Juice of 2 limes, 2 tbsp olive oil
  • 1 tbsp huacatay paste (optional but transformative)
  1. Whisk together. Coat the chickens inside and out, working marinade under the breast skin.
  2. Bag and refrigerate 12 to 24 hours, turning once.
  3. Pat dry before spitting — wet skin will never lacquer.
Al Pastor Adobo
Mexico · For 5 lb thin-sliced pork · 8–12 hr
  • 4 dried guajillo and 2 dried ancho chillies, stemmed and seeded
  • 3 tbsp achiote (annatto) paste
  • 1 cup canned pineapple juice
  • ⅓ cup white vinegar
  • 6 cloves garlic, ½ white onion
  • 1 tbsp ground cumin, 2 tsp Mexican oregano
  • 1 tsp ground cloves, 2 tbsp salt
  1. Toast the chillies briefly on the grate, then soak in hot water 20 minutes.
  2. Blend with everything else to a smooth, brick-red marinade.
  3. Layer with the sliced pork and refrigerate 8 to 12 hours.
Not longer than twelve hours

Even canned pineapple juice carries enough bromelain to break the pork down. Past about half a day the texture goes from tender to mealy and there is no fixing it.

Tandoori Double Marinade
Punjab · For 6 lb bone-in chicken · Two stages

The two-stage marinade is the whole technique. The first cuts and seasons; the second, which can only work after the first, coats and protects.

  • First marinade
  • Juice of 3 limes, 2 tbsp salt
  • 3 tbsp ginger-garlic paste
  • 1 tbsp Kashmiri chilli powder
  • Second marinade
  • 2 cups yogurt, hung in cheesecloth 4 hours until very thick
  • 3 tbsp tandoori masala (page opposite)
  • 2 tbsp mustard oil
  • 1 tbsp kasoori methi, crushed
  • 1 tbsp gram flour (optional, helps it cling)
  1. Score the chicken deeply to the bone, three slashes per piece.
  2. Rub in the first marinade and leave 30 to 45 minutes. The acid opens the meat.
  3. Coat thickly with the second marinade, working it into the slashes. Refrigerate 6 to 24 hours.
  4. Let excess drip off before grilling — a thick sheet of yogurt will steam rather than char.
