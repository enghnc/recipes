Stock, Demi-Glace and the Small Sauces

Almost nothing in classical sauce work is difficult. It is simply long, and the length is the ingredient.

The family tree

Everything in this chapter descends from one line. Roasted bones become brown stock. Brown stock thickened and reduced becomes espagnole. Espagnole reduced again with more stock becomes demi-glace. Demi-glace reduced to syrup becomes glace de viande. And a spoonful of demi-glace, dropped into a pan you have just cooked a steak in, becomes any of a dozen small sauces in about four minutes.

That last point is the whole reason to do the work. The two-day project is not something you repeat; you do it once, freeze it in cubes, and for the next six months every steak you cook can have a bordelaise on it for the cost of a shallot.

The fire advantage

Restaurants roast their bones in an oven. Roasting them over live fire instead — hard, in a single layer, with a chunk of oak on the coals — gives a stock with a depth no kitchen version has. It is the one genuine improvement a kettle makes to classical French technique, and it costs nothing but an hour you were spending outside anyway.

Fire-Roasted Brown Stock fond brun
Day one · Makes about 8 qt · 1 hr roasting, 8 hr simmer

Get the bones genuinely dark — mahogany, with black edges — because everything downstream is the colour and flavour of this step. Pale bones give a thin, grey sauce that no amount of reduction rescues.

  • 10 lb beef and veal bones — knuckle, neck and marrow, cut into 3-inch pieces
  • 2 lb beef trim or oxtail, for flavour
  • 3 onions, 4 carrots, 4 stalks celery, all in large chunks
  • 1 head garlic, halved across
  • 6 oz tomato paste
  • 2 cups dry red wine
  • 1 bunch parsley stalks · 6 sprigs thyme · 3 bay leaves · 1 tbsp peppercorns
  • Cold water to cover, about 8 quarts
  • 2 oak chunks
  1. Two-zone fire, oak on the coals, running about 425°F. Spread the bones in a single layer in a heavy foil tray on the indirect side — crowded bones steam and stay pale.
  2. Roast 45 to 60 minutes, turning twice, until deeply browned with blackened edges. Move the tray over the direct zone for the last 5 minutes if they need pushing.
  3. Add the vegetables and garlic, smear the tomato paste over everything, and roast another 20 minutes, until the paste has darkened from red to brick. Cooking the paste out is what removes its raw acidity.
  4. Tip the bones and vegetables into a stockpot. Set the tray over direct heat, pour in the red wine, and scrape every brown fragment loose — that fond is the most concentrated flavour of the whole exercise. Pour it into the pot.
  5. Cover with cold water by two inches. Bring very slowly to a bare shiver; never let it boil, which emulsifies the fat and turns the stock cloudy and greasy.
  6. Skim the grey foam for the first 30 minutes, then add the herbs and peppercorns.
  7. Simmer 8 to 12 hours, uncovered, topping up with water to keep the bones submerged. This can sit on a deflector fire at 250°F all day while you cook something else.
  8. Strain through a fine sieve without pressing on the solids. Cool fast, refrigerate overnight, and lift the set fat off the top in the morning.
KeepingFive days refrigerated, six months frozen. Freeze whatever you are not reducing in quart containers — this stock alone improves the jambalaya, the burgoo, the choucroute and every pan sauce in the book.
Espagnole
Day two · Makes about 4 qt · 2 hr

One of the five mother sauces: brown stock thickened with a dark roux and given a second vegetable base. On its own it is coarse and slightly floury. It is not meant to be eaten — it is an intermediate step, and the next reduction refines it.

  • 6 qt fire-roasted brown stock
  • 1 cup (2 sticks) butter, or the fat lifted off the stock
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 onion, 2 carrots, 2 stalks celery, finely diced
  • 4 oz tomato paste
  • 1 bay leaf, 4 sprigs thyme, 10 peppercorns
  1. Make a brown roux: melt the fat in a heavy pot over moderate direct heat, whisk in the flour, and cook, stirring constantly, 15 to 25 minutes, until it is the colour of milk chocolate and smells of toasted nuts. Do not hurry it and do not walk away — a scorched roux is bitter and unusable.
  2. In a second pan, cook the diced vegetables until deeply browned, then add the tomato paste and cook it out.
  3. Whisk the hot stock into the roux a ladle at a time, fully incorporating each addition before the next, so it never lumps.
  4. Add the vegetables and herbs. Move to the indirect side at 300°F and simmer, uncovered, 1½ to 2 hours, until reduced by about a third.
  5. Skim the skin that forms at the edge every 20 minutes — impurities collect there, and skimming is the difference between a glossy sauce and a dull one.
  6. Strain through a fine sieve.
Demi-Glace
Day two · Makes about 2 qt · 2–3 hr

Equal parts espagnole and brown stock, reduced by half. The result is glossy, deeply savoury, and just thick enough to coat a spoon — the single most useful thing in a cook's freezer.

  • 4 qt espagnole
  • 4 qt fire-roasted brown stock
  • 1 cup dry Madeira or red wine (optional, added at the end)
  1. Combine and simmer over indirect heat at 300°F, uncovered, skimming every 20 minutes.
  2. Reduce by half, 2 to 3 hours. It is ready when it coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through leaves a clean line that holds.
  3. Add the Madeira in the last 10 minutes if using, so the alcohol cooks off but the aroma stays.
  4. Strain through a fine sieve, then through muslin if you want it crystalline.
  5. Cool fast. It will set to a firm jelly in the fridge — that wobble is a sign you reduced it far enough.
Make ahead

Freeze in an ice-cube tray, then bag the cubes. One cube is roughly two tablespoons, which is exactly one portion of pan sauce. This is the entire payoff of the project: six months of four-minute sauces from two days of work.

Glace de Viande
Optional · Makes about 1½ cups from 2 qt demi
  • 2 qt demi-glace
  1. Reduce the demi-glace further, slowly, until it is syrupy and darkly glossy and coats a spoon thickly — down to roughly a twelfth of its original volume.
  2. Watch the last fifteen minutes constantly. It goes from syrup to scorched very quickly.
  3. Cool. It sets to a firm, rubbery block that keeps for months refrigerated.
  4. A teaspoon melted into any sauce, soup or braise adds depth that nothing else does. Treat it as seasoning rather than as a sauce.

The small sauces

Each of these starts in the pan the meat came out of, takes four to six minutes, and serves four. The method is always the same shape: sweat an aromatic, deglaze with an acid, add demi-glace, reduce, finish with cold butter off the heat. That last step — monter au beurre — is what gives a pan sauce its shine, and it must happen away from the heat or the sauce will split.

Sauce Bordelaise
Small sauce · Serves 4 · 10 min

The definitive steak sauce, and the reason the marrow bones in Part Nine are worth buying two extra of.

  • 3 shallots, very finely minced
  • 1½ cups dry red wine, ideally Bordeaux
  • 2 sprigs thyme · 1 bay leaf · ½ tsp cracked pepper
  • 1 cup demi-glace
  • 3 oz beef marrow, poached and diced
  • 3 tbsp cold butter, cubed · lemon · salt
  1. Sweat the shallots in a little butter in the resting pan until soft and translucent, without colouring.
  2. Add the wine, thyme, bay and pepper. Reduce hard until almost dry — a syrupy tablespoon left in the pan. This is the step people cut short, and cutting it short is why home bordelaise tastes of raw wine.
  3. Add the demi-glace and simmer 3 to 4 minutes.
  4. Strain if you want it smooth; leave the shallots in if you want it rustic.
  5. Off the heat, whisk in the cold butter a cube at a time until glossy, then fold in the diced marrow so it softens but keeps its shape.
  6. Season with salt and a few drops of lemon.
Sauce Chasseur hunter's sauce
Small sauce · Serves 4 · 12 min
  • 8 oz mushrooms, sliced · 2 shallots, minced
  • ½ cup dry white wine · 2 tbsp brandy
  • 1 cup demi-glace · 2 tomatoes, peeled and diced
  • 1 tbsp each chopped tarragon and parsley
  • 2 tbsp cold butter
  1. Sear the mushrooms hard in butter in an uncrowded pan until browned, then add the shallots.
  2. Deglaze with the brandy and let it cook off, then the white wine, reduced by two-thirds.
  3. Add the demi-glace and tomato, simmer 5 minutes.
  4. Off the heat, mount with butter and stir in the herbs. Tarragon goes in last and never simmers.
Sauce Robert & Sauce Charcutière
Small sauce · Serves 4 · 12 min

Robert is the mustard-and-onion sauce for pork. Add julienned cornichons at the end and it becomes charcutière — the two are the same sauce and the distinction is one ingredient.

  • 1 onion, finely diced · 2 tbsp butter
  • ¾ cup dry white wine · 1 tbsp white wine vinegar
  • 1 cup demi-glace
  • 2 tbsp Dijon mustard · 1 tsp sugar · black pepper
  • For charcutière: ⅓ cup cornichons, julienned
  1. Cook the onion slowly in butter until soft and lightly golden, 10 minutes.
  2. Add wine and vinegar and reduce by two-thirds, then the demi-glace, and simmer 5 minutes.
  3. Off the heat, whisk in the mustard, sugar and pepper. Mustard boiled in a sauce turns bitter and loses its edge.
  4. For charcutière, fold in the cornichons at the very end so they stay crisp.
Sauce Périgueux
Small sauce · Serves 4 · 8 min

Madeira and truffle. The classic partner for filet mignon, and the argument for cooking filet at all — a cut that needs a sauce deserves this one.

  • ½ cup dry Madeira · 1 shallot, minced
  • 1 cup demi-glace
  • 1 small black truffle, finely chopped, or 2 tbsp truffle peelings with their juice
  • 2 tbsp cold butter · salt
  1. Reduce the Madeira with the shallot until syrupy.
  2. Add the demi-glace and simmer 4 minutes.
  3. Off the heat, add the truffle and its juice, then mount with cold butter. Truffle must never boil — heat drives off exactly the aroma you paid for.
  4. If truffle is out of reach, a teaspoon of truffle oil off the heat is a shadow of it but not a disgrace.
Sauce Diable
Small sauce · Serves 4 · 10 min
  • 3 shallots, minced · ¾ cup dry white wine
  • 3 tbsp white wine vinegar · 1 tbsp cracked black peppercorns
  • 1 cup demi-glace · 1 tsp Worcestershire · pinch cayenne
  • 2 tbsp cold butter · chopped parsley
  1. Reduce the shallots, wine, vinegar and cracked pepper until nearly dry.
  2. Add demi-glace, Worcestershire and cayenne. Simmer 5 minutes.
  3. Mount with butter off the heat, finish with parsley. Sharp, peppery, and the right sauce for grilled chicken and pork.
Sauce au Poivre
Small sauce · Serves 4 · 8 min
  • 2 tbsp black peppercorns, coarsely crushed
  • 1 tbsp green peppercorns in brine, drained
  • 1 shallot, minced · 3 tbsp brandy or cognac
  • ¾ cup demi-glace · ½ cup heavy cream
  • 2 tbsp cold butter · salt
  1. Toast the crushed peppercorns in the hot pan for 30 seconds until they smell fragrant.
  2. Add the shallot, then the brandy — off the flame if you are nervous, since it may ignite. Let it reduce almost away.
  3. Add demi-glace and cream, simmer 4 to 5 minutes until it coats a spoon.
  4. Off the heat, add the green peppercorns and mount with butter.
Red Wine Jus the shortcut
Small sauce · Serves 4 · 20 min · No demi-glace required

For anyone who has not done the two-day project. It is not demi-glace and it will not have the same body, but it is a genuinely good sauce and it takes twenty minutes.

  • 3 cups good beef stock, preferably homemade
  • 1½ cups dry red wine · 3 shallots, sliced
  • 2 sprigs thyme · 1 bay leaf
  • 1 tsp tomato paste · 1 tsp red wine vinegar
  • 3 tbsp cold butter · salt
  1. Reduce the wine with the shallots, thyme, bay and tomato paste until syrupy.
  2. Add the stock and reduce hard — by about three-quarters, down to roughly a cup. This is where the body comes from, and it takes longer than you expect.
  3. Strain, sharpen with vinegar, and mount with cold butter off the heat.
  4. If it still feels thin, a teaspoon of glace de viande or a knob of butter kneaded with an equal weight of flour whisked in will thicken it without dulling the flavour.

Butter Sauces and Compound Butters

The other half of the steak repertoire, and the faster half: emulsions in fifteen minutes, and flavoured butters that take five and keep for months.

How an emulsion actually works

Hollandaise, béarnaise and beurre blanc are all the same trick — fat suspended in a small amount of water in droplets too fine to separate, held there by egg yolk lecithin or by the milk solids in butter. They break for exactly three reasons: too hot, too fast, or too much fat for the water present. Every recovery technique addresses one of those.

On a grill this matters more than indoors, because a kettle has no low setting. Make emulsions in a pan set at the edge of the fire, or in a bowl over a pot of barely-simmering water, and pull the pan off the heat the moment it thickens rather than chasing more thickness.

Rescuing a broken sauce

Broken hollandaise or béarnaise: put a tablespoon of hot water — or a fresh yolk, if it has badly split — in a clean warm bowl, and whisk the broken sauce into it a few drops at a time, as if starting over. Broken beurre blanc: take it off the heat, add an ice cube, and whisk hard. Overheated and scrambled: nothing will save it; strain out what you can, start again, and use a cooler pan.

Hollandaise
Emulsion · Serves 6 · 15 min
  • 3 egg yolks
  • 1 tbsp water · 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1 cup (2 sticks) butter, melted and warm, milk solids left behind
  • Cayenne · salt
  1. Whisk the yolks, water and lemon in a bowl over barely-simmering water until they thicken and turn pale and you can see the bottom of the bowl as the whisk passes — the ribbon stage, about 3 minutes.
  2. Off the heat, add the warm butter in a very thin stream, whisking constantly. The first third is where it breaks, so go slowest there.
  3. Season with salt and cayenne, and slacken with a teaspoon of warm water if it is too thick to pour.
  4. Hold at barely-warm — in a thermos, or a bowl set over water off the heat. Above about 150°F it scrambles.
Béarnaise
Emulsion · Serves 6 · 20 min

The correct sauce for grilled steak, and a far better use of a filet than any brown sauce. The reduction is what separates it from hollandaise, so do not shortcut it.

  • Reduction
  • ⅓ cup white wine vinegar · ⅓ cup dry white wine
  • 3 shallots, finely minced
  • 3 tbsp chopped tarragon stalks · 1 tsp cracked peppercorns
  • Sauce
  • 3 egg yolks · 1 cup (2 sticks) warm melted butter
  • 2 tbsp chopped tarragon leaves · 1 tbsp chopped chervil or parsley · salt
  1. Simmer the reduction ingredients until about 2 tablespoons of liquid remain. Strain, pressing the shallots, and cool slightly.
  2. Whisk the yolks with the reduction over barely-simmering water to the ribbon stage.
  3. Off the heat, stream in the warm butter, whisking constantly.
  4. Fold in the tarragon leaves and chervil at the end. Season.
  5. Serve warm, never hot, in a small jug or bowl beside the steak — never poured over a crust you just built.
Beurre Blanc
Emulsion · Serves 6 · 15 min

No egg at all — this one is held together by the butter's own milk solids, which makes it more fragile and more delicate. The classic sauce for grilled fish.

  • 3 shallots, very finely minced
  • ½ cup dry white wine · ¼ cup white wine vinegar
  • 2 tbsp heavy cream (optional, but it makes the emulsion far more stable)
  • 2 sticks very cold butter, cubed
  • Salt · white pepper · lemon
  1. Reduce the shallots, wine and vinegar until about 2 tablespoons remain and the pan is nearly dry.
  2. Add the cream if using and bring to a bare simmer.
  3. Move the pan to the edge of the fire and whisk in the cold butter two or three cubes at a time, never letting it boil and never adding more until the last has disappeared.
  4. The sauce should be pale, opaque and thick enough to coat a spoon. Season and sharpen with lemon.
  5. Serve within 30 minutes. It cannot be reheated — it will break and it will not come back.
Beurre rouge

Identical method with red wine and red wine vinegar in place of white. Dusty pink, slightly sharper, and better against grilled salmon or duck than the white version.

Compound butters

The fastest sauce there is: butter, flavouring, rolled in parchment and frozen. A coin laid on a resting steak melts into something indistinguishable from a pan sauce for a tenth of the effort. All of these keep three months frozen and slice straight from the freezer.

The method is the same for every one. Beat a stick of softened butter until pale, fold in the flavourings, roll tightly into a 1½-inch log in parchment, twist the ends, and chill hard. Slice into coins to serve.

Maître d'Hôtel Butter
Compound butter · Makes 1 log

The original and still the best default for grilled beef. Three ingredients and nothing to go wrong.

  • 1 stick butter, softened
  • 3 tbsp finely chopped parsley
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice · ½ tsp salt · black pepper
Café de Paris Butter
Compound butter · Makes 2 logs

The Geneva steakhouse butter, and a far longer list than it looks — but every element is doing something, and the result tastes like nothing else on a steak.

  • 2 sticks butter, softened
  • 2 anchovy fillets, mashed · 1 tbsp capers, chopped
  • 2 tbsp Dijon · 1 tbsp ketchup · 1 tsp Worcestershire
  • 2 shallots and 2 cloves garlic, very finely minced
  • 2 tbsp chopped parsley · 1 tbsp chopped tarragon · 1 tbsp chives
  • 1 tsp each sweet paprika and curry powder
  • ½ tsp dried marjoram · ½ tsp dried thyme · pinch cayenne
  • Zest and juice of ½ lemon · 2 tbsp brandy · salt
  1. Mash the anchovy and capers to a paste first, then beat everything into the softened butter.
  2. Rest it 24 hours refrigerated before rolling — the herbs and spices need a day to come together, and made fresh it tastes like a list of ingredients rather than a sauce.
  3. Roll, chill and slice thick.
Marchand de Vin Butter
Compound butter · Makes 1 log
  • 1 stick butter, softened
  • 4 shallots, minced · 1 cup dry red wine
  • 1 tsp chopped thyme · 1 tbsp parsley · salt and pepper
  1. Reduce the shallots and wine to a dry, jammy tablespoon. Cool completely — warm reduction melts the butter and the log will not set.
  2. Beat into the butter with the herbs and seasoning, roll and chill.
Anchovy, Roquefort and Bone Marrow Butters
Compound butters · One log each
  • Anchovy
  • 1 stick butter · 6 anchovy fillets, mashed · 1 clove garlic · lemon zest · black pepper
  • Roquefort
  • 1 stick butter · 3 oz Roquefort, crumbled · 1 tbsp chives · 1 tsp brandy · black pepper
  • Smoked bone marrow
  • 1 stick butter · 4 oz roasted marrow, cooled · 1 tbsp parsley · 1 tsp thyme · flaky salt
  1. Anchovy butter wants no added salt — the anchovies bring plenty.
  2. Roquefort butter should stay visibly lumpy; beating it smooth wastes the cheese.
  3. Marrow butter is the fire cook's version: roast the bones as in Part Nine, scoop the marrow, cool it, and beat it in. It is the richest thing in this book and a thin coin is enough.
To the table · plateCompound butter goes on the meat after slicing, not before resting — a coin laid on a whole steak melts down the outside and never reaches the middle. Slice, fan the slices, then lay a thin coin on every second slice so it pools into the cut surfaces.
